## Layer 2: The decision

**What's being decided:** Georgia Power splits money from real-time customers between its fuel account and its base account. [P-061] The part counted toward fuel, called the fuel credit, lowers what other customers pay for fuel. [P-144] The PSC is reviewing how that split is figured. [P-016] PIA Staff and other parties asked for the review in the fuel-rate case, and Georgia Power agreed to it. [P-053]

**What would change:**

1. **Take-or-pay contracts:** Leave both their cost and their energy out of the fuel credit ("Option 1", backed by Georgia Power and GIPL), or count both ("Option 2", backed by PIA Staff). [P-072, P-128, P-129]
2. **Pipeline fees:** Georgia Power would recover new fees tied to very large customers from those customers; PIA Staff would share all fees by energy use; GIPL would split new fees the way power-line costs are split. [P-055, P-157, P-104] GAM says no specific part of real-time revenue should be credited against these fees. [P-110]
3. **Other fuel costs:** Count costs such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead (PIA Staff and GIPL), or leave them out (Georgia Power). [P-065, P-096, P-131]
4. **Reports:** PIA Staff recommends monthly reports on the credit starting no later than Jan 1, 2027. [P-068] Georgia Power says they aren't needed and some aren't feasible in that time. [P-132]
5. **Base side:** Any change is credited to fuel, and the matching base-side change waits for Georgia Power's next base rate case. [P-016]

### How real-time pricing works

- Business customers with a peak demand of at least 250 kilowatts can choose real-time pricing (RTP). [P-044] About 2,300 use the day-ahead version (RTP-DA), and 170 use the hour-ahead version (RTP-HA), which requires at least 5 megawatts. [P-044, P-164]
- They pay standard rates for a set baseline amount of power and an hourly price for power above it. [P-043] The hourly price reflects what Georgia Power expects its marginal cost to be. [P-042] Marginal cost is the cost of making one more unit of power. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- Georgia Power says these customers take on price risk in exchange for possible savings. [P-042]
- Very large new customers pay real-time prices on all their power, plus a monthly access charge. [P-071]
- Georgia Power expects most new business growth to use real-time pricing, PIA Staff said. [P-070]
- GIPL said real-time customers' prices would not change under its plan, only how the money is counted. [P-105] Georgia Power said the same about its 2010 change to the method. [P-046] Real-time customers pay standard rates on their baseline power. [P-043] So a later base-rate change would reach that part of their bills. {analysis: follows from P-043}

### Words used on this page

- **Fuel charge:** the part of a standard-rate bill that pays for fuel; its formal name is fuel cost recovery (FCR). [P-021, P-061]
- **Base rates:** the rest of the bill, which pays for power plants, power lines, other costs besides fuel, and the company's profit. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Base rate case:** the PSC's full review of base rates. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Take-or-pay contracts:** contracts, such as Georgia Power's solar, wind and biomass deals, under which it must pay for power whenever it is produced. [P-050] The filings also call them non-dispatchable PPAs, or power purchase agreements. [P-050, P-119]
- **Pipeline fees:** fixed monthly payments to reserve space on gas pipelines for Georgia Power's power plants. [P-049, P-051] The filings call them firm transportation (FT). [P-096]
- **Peak demand and energy use:** peak demand is the most power used at one time, such as on a cold winter morning; energy use is the total power used over a month. {analysis: plain-language definitions, not from the cited sources}
- **Incremental load:** the power billed at real-time prices. [P-043]
- **GPC:** Georgia Power Company. [P-109]
- **Intervenors:** outside groups that joined the case as parties, such as GIPL and GAM. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Embedded costs:** the full average cost of running the system, as opposed to marginal cost. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **kWh and MWh:** a kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit on a power bill, and a megawatt-hour (MWh) is 1,000 of them. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}

### Fuel side versus base side

- Under the PSC's order, any change is credited to fuel, and the matching base-side change goes into a special account to be settled in the next base rate case. [P-016] The order does not say which way base rates would move. {analysis: P-016 provides for "a regulatory asset or liability"}
- Georgia Power says revenue from large customers let it freeze base rates through 2028 and will put downward pressure of at least $102 a year on a typical home's bill in 2029–2031. [P-054] That figure covers all revenue from large customers, not only the real-time money at issue here. {analysis: P-054 describes "the incremental revenue from large load customers"}
- If fuel charges had to collect $100 million less, a typical home's bill would drop by about $1.48 a month, Georgia Power estimates. [P-141, P-142] If base rates had to collect $100 million more, that bill would rise by about $1.65 a month, it estimates. {computed: P-143 gives +$1.6484 a month per +$100 million of base revenue requirement, rounded to the cent} These are examples for a home using 1,000 kWh a month, based on its recent storm-cost and fuel-rate changes. [P-141] They are not forecasts for any plan. {analysis: P-141 describes a $100 million change, not a plan}
- No filing we read gives the net effect of any plan on a typical home's total bill. {analysis: Staff's $1.35 (P-066, P-067) covers the fuel charge only; P-016 and P-090 leave base effects to a base rate case; P-142 and P-143 are per-$100 million examples}
- GAM says a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates." [P-115] GIPL says its plan "may increase base rates for those same customers, depending on how other cost allocation questions are handled." [P-105] PIA Staff says "the direction and magnitude of any future impact would need to be determined in a base rate case." ## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

Below are today's method and the three plans in the testimony we could read. {editorial: process note; RSM's testimony is a scanned image that could not be copied into passages} The PSC can also mix parts of them. {analysis: the order says the PSC "may make modifications if appropriate" (P-016)} The plans use letters; Option 1 and Option 2 are only the two take-or-pay choices. {editorial}

### Plan A: Keep today's method

- **What it does:** Counts real-time money toward fuel at the "average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost" of Georgia Power's running plants, plus its share of "pool sales or purchases." [P-048] Fixed fuel costs, such as take-or-pay contracts and pipeline fees, stay out of that rate. [P-049]
- **Where it stands:** Among the four parties whose testimony we could read, none asks to keep it unchanged. {analysis: Georgia Power (P-055, P-058), PIA Staff (P-073, P-084) and GIPL (P-095, P-096) propose changes; GAM (P-111) accepts one only if the PSC finds it is needed} GAM says real-time revenue already lowers other customers' rates, and it accepts either of Georgia Power's take-or-pay options only if the PSC finds a change is needed. [P-111]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates, through base rates, Georgia Power and GAM say: real-time money not counted toward fuel helps pay fixed costs on the base side. [P-124, P-111] Georgia Power estimates that one part of that money, which it calls capital substitution, came to $264 million in 2025. [P-052] PIA Staff estimates that under its plan, Georgia Power's return on equity, a measure of its profit, would have been about half a percentage point lower in 2023–2025. [P-091]
- **Who pays:** PIA Staff says customers on standard rates absorb the fixed fuel costs left out of the credit. [P-063] PIA Staff estimates that Option 2 would have added $70 million, $74 million and $76 million to the credit in 2023, 2024 and 2025. [P-078] Staff says its data were incomplete, so these figures may be too low. [P-078]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether the real-time money on the base side makes up for the fuel costs left out. {editorial} Georgia Power and GAM say real-time money on the base side already helps pay fixed costs, including pipeline fees. [P-124, P-110] GIPL agrees in concept, but says this ignores that fuel and base costs are shared among customer groups differently and that fuel balances carry forward. [P-099]

### Plan B: Georgia Power's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 1, leaving out both the cost and the energy of take-or-pay contracts. [P-058, P-128] New pipeline fees caused by very large customers would be recovered from them, split by each group's share of growth in winter peak demand. [P-055, P-056] Existing pipeline fees and other left-out costs would stay out of the credit. [P-065, P-131]
- **Where it stands:** Georgia Power proposes it and prefers Option 1. [P-119, P-128] GIPL also backs Option 1, and agrees new pipeline fees tied to large customers should come out of the fuel charge, but it wants a different split. [P-128, P-101] GAM says that if a change is needed, either take-or-pay option would "adequately address" the concerns. [P-111] GAM says Georgia Power's pipeline plan would make very large customers fully cover their new pipeline fees. [P-113]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit than today. [P-058, P-115] Georgia Power says its pipeline split protects other customers even if planned large projects fall through. [P-127]
- **Who pays:** Less real-time money goes toward base costs, which could raise base rates later; the parties differ on how much, as Layer 2 explains. [P-115, P-105, P-090] Very large customers' payments would cover the new pipeline fees tied to their growth. [P-055]

### 1. Leave take-or-pay contracts out entirely (Option 1)

- **What's uncertain:** Whether keeping real-time pricing tied to marginal cost matters more than counting every cost that serves all customers; this is mainly a question of values. {editorial}

### 2. Count the costs of take-or-pay contracts too (Option 2)

Documented · Side: favors Option 2 · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (premise: the contracts serve all customers, so their costs belong in the credit; PIA Staff says so, while Georgia Power and GIPL say their costs and benefits belong with customers on standard rates); Uncertain on size (PIA Staff's estimate is the only public figure; Georgia Power's estimates are blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says the contracts serve all customers, so their costs should count when the fuel credit is figured. [P-080] Georgia Power and GIPL disagree, and the size of the change rests on Staff's estimate alone. [P-129, P-100, P-078, P-139]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn, in testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said: "These costs are incurred to serve all customers at all times and should be included when the Company calculates the amount of RTP revenues credited to fuel revenues." [P-060, P-160, P-161, P-059, P-080]
- **What supports it:** PIA Staff said Option 2 "is consistent with the 2010 rate case proposal, which assumed that all Georgia power unit and purchase costs would be included in the calculation." [P-073] In 2025, about 9.8 million MWh of take-or-pay energy was counted in the calculation while the related $486 million in costs was not, PIA Staff said. [P-076] That $486 million is the contracts' total cost for the year, not the share real-time customers would carry. [P-077] Staff said the excluded contracts may top 17 million MWh by 2027, "showing that the impacts of non-dispatchable PPAs are growing." [P-077] PIA Staff estimates that Option 2 would have added $220 million to the credit in 2023–2025, and says its data were incomplete, so the figure may be too low. [P-078] Georgia Power's own direct testimony said Option 2 would "result in a larger allocation of RTP revenues to the fuel clause." [P-058, P-128]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said Option 2 "would be a departure from the marginal fuel cost-based approach approved by the Commission and in place since 2010." [P-129] GIPL said leaving the contracts out is "appropriate, as incremental RTP load should be assigned the cost of resources that may be dispatched in response to price-sensitive demand." [P-100]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said real-time customers cannot avoid take-or-pay costs by cutting their use. [P-130] PIA Staff's answer is that the energy from these contracts serves all customers. [P-074]
- **What's missing:** Georgia Power's own estimates for both options are blacked out in the public copy. [P-138, P-139, P-140] So the public copies we found have no dollar figure for Option 1 to compare with Staff's Option 2 figures. {analysis: P-139 is fully blacked out; Staff's figures (P-078, P-079) cover Option 2 only}
- **What's uncertain:** How much the credit would change, since Staff's figures are its own estimates and Georgia Power's are blacked out. [P-078, P-139, P-140] Whether counting every cost that serves all customers matters more than keeping real-time pricing tied to marginal cost; this is mainly a question of values. {editorial}

### 3. Recover new pipeline fees from very large customers, split by winter peak (Georgia Power's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors Georgia Power's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (premise: pipeline fees are driven by peak demand, and new ones mainly by very large customers; Georgia Power says so, and GIPL's filed testimony also ties the fees to peak demand, while PIA Staff says they are needed in every hour); Uncertain on size (the new-fee forecast is blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Georgia Power says new pipeline fees are driven by peak demand from very large new customers, so those customers should cover them, split by winter peak growth. [P-051, P-126, P-055, P-056] PIA Staff and GIPL disagree with this split. [P-085, P-101]
- **Who says it:** Georgia Power witnesses Ross Beppler, Lee Evans and Brandon Looney, in direct testimony filed Aug 14, 2026, proposed that new pipeline fees caused by large customers "be fully recovered from those customers and not borne by non-large load customers in the FCR clause." [P-041, P-055] In rebuttal testimony filed Oct 27, 2026, they said energy use "is not an appropriate allocation factor for FT because FT is fixed, driven by peak demand, and should be treated similarly to other large infrastructure expenses (generation, transmission, distribution) and allocated on a demand basis." [P-117, P-126]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power said "the growth of large load customers is a primary driver of new FT costs," while existing pipeline fees "are not driven by large load growth." [P-051] It said its split builds on PIA Staff's own peak-based showing in the fuel case: "The Company agrees with this approach but proposes using a winter CP." [P-056] Georgia Power said its split is fixed in advance, so very large customers cover the new fees even if their projects don't materialize. [P-127] GIPL's filed testimony also said pipeline space "is required to meet peak demand conditions." [P-104] GAM said the plan "would ensure that incremental FT costs attributable to new very large load customers are fully recovered from these customers." [P-113]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said: "FT is necessary for all hours across each month in order to reliably serve customers." [P-085] GIPL agreed that taking new large-customer fees out of the fuel charge "addresses one problem," but it rejected the winter-peak split and said real-time load should not be excused from "an explicit allocation of FT cost." [P-101]
- **What challenges it:** PIA Staff said the plan leaves out existing pipeline fees and "adds undue complexity." [P-082] Georgia Power's reply is that existing fees are not driven by large-customer growth. [P-051] GIPL said Georgia Power has not let intervenors review the large-load cost model behind the split, "not even on a trade secret basis." [P-103] Georgia Power said its split is "consistent with the capacity need that drove the incremental generation resources and the allocation in the large load cost model." [P-056]
- **What's missing:** The dollar amounts in Georgia Power's forecast of new pipeline fees are blacked out in the public copy. [P-146]
- **What's uncertain:** How much new pipeline cost very large customers will cause, since those amounts are not public. {analysis: P-146} How to split fixed costs between customer groups is partly a values question. {editorial}

### 4. Share all pipeline and storage fees by energy use (PIA Staff's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors PIA Staff's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (premise: pipeline fees serve every hour, and sharing them by energy use avoids collecting them twice; Georgia Power disputes both, and GIPL's filed testimony ties the fees to peak demand); Uncertain on size (PIA Staff's yearly estimates are blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says pipeline and storage fees are needed in every hour, so all of them should be shared by monthly energy use, real-time customers included. [P-085, P-157] Georgia Power and GAM disagree with sharing the fees this way. [P-126, P-127, P-110]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn, in testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said an energy split "is consistent with the manner in which fuel costs are recovered" and with "how FT costs have been recovered from non-RTP customers over the past 15 years." [P-059, P-060, P-160, P-161, P-084]
- **What supports it:** Staff's reason is double recovery: Georgia Power says real-time money on the base side helps cover fixed costs like pipeline fees, while "all FT costs are indisputably charged to and recovered from fuel revenues." [P-083, P-124, P-052] Staff said its plan is meant "to eliminate this risk." [P-084] PIA Staff said real-time customers use power "in both high and low load hours." [P-081] GIPL said splitting fees by energy or by peak demand gives different results for different customer groups. [P-102] Georgia Power's rebuttal says GIPL's witness moved to an energy-based split at a hearing, after receiving Georgia Power's later data responses. [P-154, P-155]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said an energy split "does not provide this level of customer protection" if large projects fall through. [P-127] It said it would only collect twice "if it was charging customers for FT costs in both fuel and base rates," and that pipeline fees are not in base rates. [P-123] GAM said "No specific portion of RTP revenues should be credited against FT costs," while real-time sales already help recover them when system lambda, the part of the real-time price that reflects the replacement cost of fuel, "exceeds GPC’s average fuel costs." [P-110] GAM also said very large new customers will pay access charges for their added costs, so pipeline costs are "entirely unrelated to RTP." [P-168]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said Staff's energy split "cannot be reconciled" with Staff's fuel-case testimony five months earlier, which used a peak-based method. [P-125] PIA Staff's reply is that it used that method "simply for illustration purposes." [P-145]
- **What's missing:** PIA Staff's yearly estimates for its pipeline plan are blacked out in the public copy. [P-147]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether real-time money on the base side already helps pay these fees, as Georgia Power and GAM say. [P-124, P-110] GIPL called that "questionable." [P-148]

### 5. Split new pipeline fees the way power-line costs are split (GIPL's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors GIPL's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (premise: pipeline fees, like power-line costs, serve peak demand whenever it occurs; Georgia Power also ties them to peak demand, while PIA Staff says they are needed in every hour); Uncertain on size (the new-fee forecast is blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** GIPL says new pipeline fees should be split the way power-line costs are, possibly as a step until the next rate case, and the credit should include a direct pipeline share. [P-104, P-096] Georgia Power and PIA Staff each prefer their own split. [P-056, P-157]
- **Who says it:** GIPL witness John D. Wilson, in direct testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said pipeline space "is similar to transmission, in that it is required to meet peak demand conditions whenever and for whatever reason they may occur." [P-092, P-152, P-104]
- **What supports it:** GIPL said splitting fees by energy or by peak demand gives different results for different customer groups, so where the money is credited matters. [P-102] It said it is not clear whether Georgia Power's capacity values already include pipeline costs. [P-148] It asked the PSC to have Georgia Power share a working copy of its large-load cost model with intervenors. [P-096]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power proposes a winter-peak split, which it says fits "the capacity need that drove the incremental generation resources." [P-056] PIA Staff said: "FT is necessary for all hours across each month in order to reliably serve customers." [P-085]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power's rebuttal says GIPL's witness changed his recommendation to an energy-based split at a hearing, after receiving Georgia Power's later data responses. [P-154, P-155]
- **What's missing:** The new-fee forecast is blacked out, and the Oct 15 hearing transcript is listed without a file. [P-146, P-003]
- **What's uncertain:** Which split GIPL now backs, and whether Georgia Power's capacity values already cover pipeline costs. {analysis: P-154, P-155 and P-148}

### 6. Count more fuel-related costs in the credit (PIA Staff and GIPL)

Documented · Side: favors counting more costs (PIA Staff and GIPL) · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (premise: the costs left out fall on customers on standard rates; PIA Staff and GIPL say so, while Georgia Power says real-time customers pay toward fixed costs through the base side); whether each cost belongs in the credit is not mainly a factual question <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff and GIPL say fuel-related costs such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead are left out of the credit, so customers on standard rates carry them. [P-063, P-099] Georgia Power asks the PSC not to add them. [P-131]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn, in testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said leaving out these costs "results in non-RTP customers absorbing all these costs." [P-059, P-060, P-160, P-161, P-063] GIPL witness John D. Wilson, in direct testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said the credit is "an underestimate of the fuel costs that should be allocated to the incremental load priced at RTP." [P-092, P-152, P-099]
- **What supports it:** PIA Staff said costs like rail car leases depend on how much fuel, or what kind, the company buys. [P-149] Staff based its view on three guidelines, including that fuel costs should be recovered from all customers based on energy use. [P-062] GIPL said coal-related costs rise and fall with lasting changes in demand. [P-156] GIPL said parties in the fuel case raised concerns that the current method, "which has been applied to data center customers, resulted in higher fuel costs for other customers, in apparent contravention of Georgia Power’s statements that data centers would not be subsidized by existing customers." [P-097]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said the PSC "should decline to adopt PIA Staff’s recommendations presented in Table 1." [P-131] It said Staff's guidelines "are not based on or consistent with cost-allocation best practices, previous Commission policy decisions, or any widely accepted principles." [P-120] It said "the RTP rate is by design a marginal cost-based rate that is not intended to recover all embedded costs," but "This does not mean that RTP customers do not pay their fair share of system costs or cost to serve." [P-121] It said it "has committed to protect existing customers from bearing any of the costs of adding these large customers." [P-054]
- **What challenges it:** The two supporters split on some items: GIPL would leave gas resale gains and losses out, while PIA Staff would count them. [P-106, P-087] PIA Staff said it does not necessarily expect every left-out cost to be included, since "policy and cost causation arguments" could justify leaving some out. [P-064]
- **What's missing:** In PIA Staff's public table of the costs left out of the credit, most single items are blacked out. [P-150] The yearly totals are public: about $741 million in 2023, $824 million in 2024 and $732 million in 2025. {computed: P-150 totals $740,969,011, $824,063,892 and $732,060,350, rounded to the nearest million} Staff says only a portion of these totals would be credited to fuel under its plan. [P-150]
- **What's uncertain:** Which costs should count as fuel costs at all; this is mainly a question of how to define them. {editorial}

### 7. Limit the shift of real-time money to fuel (GAM)

Documented · Side: favors limiting the shift to fuel; accepts either of Georgia Power's take-or-pay options if the PSC finds a change is needed (GAM) · Claim type: factual and predictive · Evidence: Supported that a bigger fuel credit leaves less real-time money for base costs (the PSC's order and all four parties' testimony); Uncertain whether and how much base rates would rise (a future base rate case decides; GAM's 2025 total is blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** GAM says real-time money already lowers other customers' base costs, so a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates." [P-111, P-115] Whether base rates would rise, and by how much, would be settled in a future base rate case. [P-016, P-090]
- **Who says it:** GAM witness Jonathan Ly, in testimony filed Sep 23, 2026, said "any increase in the amount of RTP revenues credited to GPC’s fuel account will necessarily reduce the RTP revenues currently used to offset GPC’s base revenue requirements." [P-153, P-111] Georgia Power's witnesses said in direct testimony filed Aug 14, 2026, that real-time revenue above added costs puts "downward pressure on rates for all customers." [P-041, P-045]
- **What supports it:** GAM said real-time revenue cut other customers' base revenues by $214 million in Georgia Power's 2022 rate case, and that the 2025 total, which includes $264 million from system lambda, is "a significant increase." [P-114] A Georgia Power cost study cited by GAM shows homes pay 58.07% of base revenues and 55.36% of fuel revenues. [P-116] Georgia Power's direct testimony said changing the method would change base rate revenue. [P-047]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said "since the fuel costs are completely the responsibility of ratepayers and the Company has no financial exposure, it is critical to get the RTP fuel credit methodology correct." [P-090] It said any base-rate impact "would be a natural consequence of revising the methodology to accurately match costs and cost recovery in the same mechanism." [P-090] GIPL said it agrees "with this concept," but that it "neglects two critical factors": fuel and base costs are shared among customer groups differently, and fuel over- or under-collections carry forward as a balance. [P-099] GIPL also said the base-side money "does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098]
- **What challenges it:** PIA Staff estimates Georgia Power could have absorbed the larger fuel credit in 2023–2025 while staying within the profit range the PSC allows. [P-090, P-091]
- **What's missing:** GAM's 2025 total for real-time money on the base side is blacked out in the public version. [P-112, P-114]
- **What's uncertain:** PIA Staff said "the direction and magnitude of any future impact would need to be determined in a base rate case." [P-090] The PSC's order leaves the timing to Georgia Power's next base rate case. [P-016]

### Questions to weigh

- Should part of real-time customers' payments count toward fixed fuel costs that serve all customers but don't change with hour-to-hour use, or is the money now counted on the base side enough? {editorial}
- How should the PSC weigh a lower fuel charge against a possible change in base rates, whose size and direction a future base rate case would decide? {editorial}
- How should pipeline and gas storage fees, old and new, be shared among real-time customers, very large new customers and everyone else, and should they be split by peak demand or by energy use? {editorial}
