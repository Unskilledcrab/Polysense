## Layer 2: The decision

**What's being decided:** Georgia Power splits real-time customers' payments between fuel and base costs. [P-061] The fuel part, called the fuel credit, lowers what other customers pay for fuel; the rest helps cover base costs. [P-144, P-061, P-124] The PSC is reviewing that split. [P-016]

**What would change:**

1. **Take-or-pay contracts** (power Georgia Power must buy whenever it is produced): today the credit counts their energy but not their cost. [P-050, P-049, P-076] Count both, or neither? {editorial} Either raises the credit. [P-058, P-115]
2. **Pipeline fees** (fixed payments to reserve gas pipeline space): should real-time money count against them, and how? [P-051, P-081, P-055, P-157, P-104, P-110]
3. **Other fuel costs**, such as coal rail car leases: should the credit count them? [P-063, P-065]
4. **Reports:** should Georgia Power file monthly reports on the credit? [P-068, P-132]

One party's testimony, Resource Supply Management's, is a scanned image we could not read, so this page does not cover its view. {editorial: process note}

### How real-time pricing works

- Business customers with a demand of at least 250 kilowatts can choose real-time pricing (RTP). [P-044, P-042] About 2,300 use the day-ahead version (RTP-DA), and about 170 use the hour-ahead version (RTP-HA), which requires at least 5 megawatts. [P-044, P-164]
- They pay standard rates, fuel charge included, on a set baseline amount of power, and an hourly price on power above it. [P-043, P-048] The hourly price reflects what Georgia Power expects its marginal cost to be. [P-042] Marginal cost is the cost of making one more unit of power. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- So later changes to either the fuel charge or base rates would reach real-time customers' baseline power; new large-load customers on one tariff have no baseline. {analysis: follows from P-043, P-048 and P-071}
- Georgia Power says these customers take on price risk in exchange for possible savings. [P-042]
- Georgia Power expects most new business growth to use real-time pricing, PIA Staff said. [P-070] GIPL says the method under review has been applied to data centers. [P-097]
- PIA Staff says new large-load customers on one rate schedule (tariff) have no baseline, pay real-time rates and pay a monthly access charge to cover added fixed costs. [P-071] GAM says new customers of 100 megawatts and up may choose real-time pricing for all or part of their power, and pay access charges that reflect Georgia Power's added costs. [P-168] The two descriptions may cover different groups. {analysis: P-071 describes customers on one tariff; P-168 describes customers of 100 MW and up}
- GIPL says real-time customers' prices would not change under its plan, only how the money is counted, and Georgia Power said the same of its 2010 change to the method. [P-105, P-046]

### Words used on this page

- **Fuel charge:** the part of a standard-rate bill that pays for fuel; its formal name is fuel cost recovery (FCR). [P-021, P-061]
- **Base rates:** the rest of the bill, which pays for power plants, power lines, other costs besides fuel, and the company's profit. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Base rate case:** the PSC's full review of base rates. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Regulatory asset or liability:** an amount the company records to collect from customers, or return to them, later; here the next base rate case settles it. {analysis: plain-language gloss of P-016}
- **Take-or-pay contracts:** contracts, such as Georgia Power's solar, wind and biomass deals, under which it must pay for power whenever it is produced. [P-050] The filings also call them non-dispatchable PPAs, or power purchase agreements. [P-050, P-119]
- **Pipeline fees:** fixed monthly payments to reserve space on gas pipelines for Georgia Power's power plants. [P-049, P-051] The filings call them firm transportation (FT). [P-096]
- **Peak demand and energy use:** peak demand is the most power used at one time, such as on a cold winter morning; energy use is the total power used over a month. {analysis: plain-language definitions, not from the cited sources}
- **Average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost:** the rate the credit uses today. [P-048] It is the cost to replace the fuel burned, averaged over all of Georgia Power's units running that hour. {analysis: plain-language gloss of P-048}
- **Pool sales or purchases:** power traded through a power pool; the credit's rate counts Georgia Power's share. {analysis: plain-language gloss; P-048 names it}
- **System lambda:** the cost of the last unit of power the system makes in an hour. [P-052] It is part of the real-time price, and Georgia Power says it is above the average running cost in nearly all hours. [P-052]
- **Capital substitution:** Georgia Power's name for part of the gap between lambda and the money recorded to fuel; it goes to base rates. [P-052]
- **Capacity values:** the part of the real-time price meant to cover having enough power plants for peak times. {analysis: plain-language gloss of the "reliability capacity cost component" and "marginal value of capacity" in P-148}
- **Large-load customers:** the parties' term for big new power users; the filings we read give it no size threshold. {editorial: process note; P-051, P-055, P-071 and P-101 use the term without one} GAM's "very large load" means new customers of 100 megawatts and up. [P-168]
- **Under-collected fuel balance:** fuel costs already spent but not yet collected through the fuel charge. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Incremental load:** the power billed at real-time prices. [P-043]
- **GPC:** Georgia Power Company. [P-109]
- **Embedded costs:** the full average cost of running the system, as opposed to marginal cost. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **kW, MW, kWh and MWh:** a kilowatt (kW) measures power used at one moment, and a megawatt (MW) is 1,000 kilowatts. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources} A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit on a power bill, and a megawatt-hour (MWh) is 1,000 of them. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}

[P-016] The order does not say how base rates would then move. {analysis: P-016 provides for either an asset or a liability}
- Georgia Power estimates that each $100 million is about $1.48 a month on a typical home's fuel charge, or about $1.65 a month on its base rates. ### Plan A: Keep today's method

- **What it does:** Counts real-time money toward fuel at the "average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost" of Georgia Power's running plants, plus its share of "pool sales or purchases." [P-048] It counts take-or-pay contracts' energy but not their cost, and leaves pipeline fees out. [P-049, P-058, P-076]
- **Where it stands:** Georgia Power, PIA Staff and GIPL propose changes. [P-055, P-058, P-073, P-095] GAM says no specific part of real-time revenue should be credited against pipeline fees, and that a bigger credit "would put upward pressure on base rates." [P-110, P-115] It says either take-or-pay option "would adequately address" the concerns if the PSC finds a change is needed. [P-111] Georgia Power says its real-time rate is not meant to recover all embedded costs, but that this "does not mean that RTP customers do not pay their fair share of system costs." [P-121] PIA Staff says counting the take-or-pay contracts' energy but not their cost "unreasonably suppressed" the credit. [P-076]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates, through lower base costs when base rates are next set, Georgia Power and GAM say; base rates are frozen through 2028. [P-124, P-111, P-054] Georgia Power estimates that capital substitution, one part of that base-side money, came to $264 million in 2025. [P-052] That is the whole amount, not what any plan would move. {analysis: compare P-052 with P-078 and P-079} GIPL says capital substitution is "a necessary cost of service, and from an economics perspective does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098]
- **Who pays:** PIA Staff and GIPL say customers on the fuel charge carry the fixed fuel costs left out of the credit; GIPL says this "shifts costs to FCR customers." [P-063, P-099] PIA Staff estimates that Option 2 would have added $70 million, $74 million and $76 million to the credit in 2023, 2024 and 2025, compared with today's method. [P-078] Under today's method, the same amounts stayed on the base side. {computed: dollar for dollar, as P-061 describes the split}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether the real-time money on the base side makes up for the fuel costs left out. {editorial} Georgia Power says it helps pay fixed costs such as pipeline fees, GAM says it can, and GIPL agrees with the idea but says it "neglects two critical factors." [P-124, P-110, P-099]

### Plan B: Georgia Power's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 1, removing both the cost and the energy of take-or-pay contracts. [P-058, P-128] New pipeline fees tied to large-load customers would be recovered from them, based on their share of growth in winter peak demand. - **Where it stands:** Georgia Power proposes it and prefers Option 1. [P-119, P-128] GIPL also backs Option 1 and taking new large-load pipeline fees out of the fuel charge. [P-128, P-096] But it rejects the winter-peak split and would count coal and overhead costs. [P-101, P-096] PIA Staff opposes Option 1 and calls the pipeline plan "only a partial solution" that "adds undue complexity," though it also counts new pipeline fees in the credit. [P-074, P-082, P-065] GAM says that if a change is needed, either take-or-pay option would "adequately address" the concerns. [P-111]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit, from Option 1 and from new pipeline fees counted against large-load customers' payments. [P-058, P-055] How much is unknown, since Georgia Power's estimates are blacked out. [P-049] Of the base-side argument, GIPL says, "While I agree with this concept, it neglects two critical factors." [P-099] It says capital substitution is "a necessary cost of service, and from an economics perspective does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098] Georgia Power's rebuttal says that real-time fuel revenue not fully covering pipeline fees "does not mean that RTP customers are not effectively paying their share of fixed costs with the portion of RTP revenue that is allocated to base." [P-124]
- **What's missing:** Most single amounts in PIA Staff's table of left-out costs are blacked out. [P-150]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether costs that do not change with hour-to-hour use belong in the fuel credit; this is partly a question of definitions and partly of values. {editorial}

### 9. Limit the shift of real-time money to fuel (GAM)

Documented · Side: favors limiting the shift to fuel (GAM) · Claim type: factual, predictive and values · Evidence: Supported (no party whose testimony we could read disputes that a bigger fuel credit moves the same dollars off the base side); Uncertain (how base rates would move: a later base rate case decides); size (the same dollars as the fuel side, such as PIA Staff's $220 million for Option 2 in 2023–2025) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** GAM says a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates," because real-time money now lowers other customers' base costs. [P-115, P-111] GIPL and PIA Staff accept a base-side effect, but GIPL calls part of that money a "surplus" that "is needed" and "does not provide further surplus" to lower rates, and Staff says a later base rate case would set its direction and size. [P-105, P-098, P-090]
- **Who says it:** GAM witness Jonathan Ly filed testimony on Sep 23, 2026. [P-153] Ly said "any increase in the amount of RTP revenues credited to GPC’s fuel account will necessarily reduce the RTP revenues currently used to offset GPC’s base revenue requirements." [P-111]
- **What supports it:** No party whose testimony we could read disputes that a bigger fuel credit moves the same dollars off the base side. {analysis: P-061 (PIA Staff), P-047 (Georgia Power), P-105 (GIPL) and P-111 (GAM)} GIPL, which backs a bigger credit, says its plan "will assist in reducing FCR rates for FCR customers, but may increase base rates for those same customers, depending on how other cost allocation questions are handled." [P-105] Georgia Power's direct testimony said real-time revenue above added costs puts "downward pressure on rates for all customers," and that changing the method would change base rate revenue. [P-045, P-047] GAM said real-time revenue cut other customers' base revenues by $214 million in Georgia Power's 2022 rate case. [P-114] Citing Georgia Power, GAM said system lambda contributed $264 million in 2025, and that the 2025 total is "a significant increase." [P-114]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff accepts a base-rate effect as "a natural consequence of revising the methodology to accurately match costs and cost recovery in the same mechanism." [P-090] It said "since the fuel costs are completely the responsibility of ratepayers and the Company has no financial exposure, it is critical to get the RTP fuel credit methodology correct." [P-090] GIPL agrees "with this concept," but says it "neglects two critical factors": fuel and base costs are shared among customer groups differently, and fuel balances carry forward. [P-099]
- **What challenges it:** Looking back, PIA Staff says Georgia Power could have absorbed its plan "while maintaining its targeted Return on Equity," with estimated earned returns of 10.3, 10.9 and 10.9 percent in 2023–2025, "well within" the PSC's allowed range of 9.5 to 11.9 percent. [P-090, P-091] For any change made now, the order records the base-side change for the next base rate case. [P-016] GIPL said capital substitution is "a necessary cost of service, and from an economics perspective does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098] Georgia Power's rebuttal says this money "is reducing other costs recovered through base rates and effectively lowering the retail cost of service for customers in a base rate case." [P-124]
- **What's missing:** GAM's 2025 total for real-time money on the base side is blacked out in the public version. [P-112, P-114]
- **What's uncertain:** How much base rates would move, and which way: the PSC's order leaves that to Georgia Power's next base rate case. [P-016] Whether a lower fuel charge now is worth a possible base-rate change later is partly a values question. {editorial}

### Questions to weigh

- For take-or-pay contracts, should the credit count both their cost and their energy (Option 2), neither (Option 1), or keep today's energy-only count? {editorial}
- How should the PSC weigh a lower fuel charge now against possible base-rate effects later, which its order leaves to the next base rate case? {editorial}
- Should any real-time revenue be counted toward pipeline and gas storage fees? {editorial} If so, should old and new fees be treated alike, split by peak demand or by energy use, or moved to base rates? {editorial}
- Do real-time customers already pay their share of fixed fuel costs through the base side, or should the fuel credit count those costs too? {editorial}
- Customer groups pay different shares of fuel costs and base costs. [P-116, P-099] How would each plan affect homes compared with businesses? {editorial}
- Which of these changes belong in this case, and which in Georgia Power's next base rate case? {editorial}
