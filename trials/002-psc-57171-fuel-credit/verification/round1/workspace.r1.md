## 30-second version

- **What's being decided:** Some big businesses, including data centers, buy power from Georgia Power at hourly real-time prices. [P-042, P-044, P-097] The Public Service Commission (PSC) will decide how much of that money counts toward fuel costs. [P-013, P-016] Homes and small businesses pay fuel costs through a separate charge on their bills. [P-061, P-144]
- **Who it affects:** Homes and businesses on Georgia Power's standard rates. [P-061] Counting more of the money toward fuel would lower their fuel charge. [P-066, P-105] But less would then go toward the company's other costs, so base rates could rise later. [P-016, P-105, P-115] The big customers' own prices would not change. [P-105]
- **What's next:** The PSC holds a hearing on Nov 12 and plans to decide on Dec 15, 2026. [P-010, P-013] You can speak at the hearing as a public witness or send a written comment before the vote. [P-025, P-159, P-030]

## Layer 2: The decision

**What's being decided:** Big businesses on real-time pricing pay one hourly price for their extra power. [P-043, P-061] Georgia Power must split that money between its fuel account and its base account. [P-061] The part counted toward fuel, called the fuel credit, lowers the fuel costs other customers pay. [P-144] The rest goes toward base costs. [P-061] The PSC is reviewing how this split is figured. [P-016] PIA Staff and other parties asked for the review as more large customers joined real-time pricing. [P-053, P-069]

**How real-time pricing works:**

- Business customers that use at least 250 kilowatts can choose it. [P-044] About 2,300 customers use the day-ahead version and 170 use the hour-ahead version. [P-044]
- They pay standard rates for a set baseline amount of power and an hourly price for power above it. [P-043]
- The hourly price reflects what Georgia Power expects its marginal cost to be. [P-042] Marginal cost is the cost of making one more unit of power. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- Very large new customers pay real-time prices on all their power, plus a monthly access charge. [P-071] GIPL says the method under review "has been applied to data center customers." [P-097]
- Georgia Power expects most new business growth to use real-time pricing, PIA Staff said. [P-070]

**Terms in the quotes:**

- RTP means real-time pricing, and FCR means fuel cost recovery, the fuel charge. [P-021]
- FT means firm transportation, the pipeline fees. [P-051, P-096]
- Non-dispatchable PPAs, or power purchase agreements, are the take-or-pay contracts. [P-050, P-119]
- Incremental load is the power billed at real-time prices. [P-043] GPC means Georgia Power Company. [P-109]
- A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit on a power bill, and a megawatt-hour (MWh) is 1,000 of them. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}

**What would change:**

1. **Take-or-pay contracts:** Georgia Power must pay for power from these contracts, such as its solar, wind and biomass deals, whenever it is produced. [P-050] Today their energy counts in the fuel credit math, but their cost does not. [P-057, P-076] "Option 1" would leave out both, and "Option 2" would count both. [P-072, P-128] Either one would raise the fuel credit. [P-115, P-128]
2. **Pipeline fees:** Georgia Power pays fixed monthly fees to reserve space on gas pipelines for its power plants. [P-049, P-051] Georgia Power would recover the new fees tied to very large new customers' growth from those customers, not from other customers' fuel charge. [P-055] PIA Staff would share all pipeline and gas storage fees among all customers by energy use, real-time customers included. [P-157]
3. **Other costs:** PIA Staff and GIPL would also count costs such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead. [P-087, P-088, P-096] Georgia Power would not. [P-131]
4. **Reports:** PIA Staff wants new monthly reports on these credits starting no later than Jan 1, 2027. [P-068] Georgia Power says the reports aren't needed. [P-132]
5. **What stays the same:** The real-time customers' own prices would not change, only how their payments are counted. [P-105, P-046]

### Fuel side versus base side

- Under the PSC's order, any change is credited to fuel. [P-016] The matching change on the base side goes into a special account and is settled in Georgia Power's next base rate case. [P-016] A base rate case is the PSC's full review of base rates. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- Georgia Power says its base rates are frozen through 2028. [P-054]
- If fuel charges had to collect $100 million less, a typical home's bill would drop by about $1.48 a month, Georgia Power estimates. [P-141, P-142] If base rates had to collect $100 million more, that bill would rise by about $1.65 a month, it estimates. {computed: P-143 gives +$1.6484 a month per +$100 million of base revenue requirement, rounded to the cent} Its typical home uses 1,000 kWh a month. [P-141]
- So moving money from the base side to the fuel side does not simply cut bills, because base rates could rise later. {analysis: P-016 sends the base-side change to the next base rate case; GIPL (P-105) and GAM (P-115) say base rates may rise} PIA Staff says Georgia Power could have absorbed the change in 2023–2025 while staying within the profit range the PSC allows. [P-090, P-091]
- Homes pay 58.07% of base revenues but 55.36% of fuel revenues, by a cost study GAM cited. [P-116] GAM says that is why a bigger fuel credit would help medium and large businesses more than homes and small businesses. [P-116]

## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

These are the four plans in the record. {editorial} The PSC can also mix parts of them. {analysis: the order says the PSC "may make modifications if appropriate" (P-016)} Georgia Power's names for its two take-or-pay choices, Option 1 and Option 2, are kept; the plans below use letters instead. {editorial}

### Option A: Keep today's method

- **What it does:** Counts real-time money toward fuel using an hourly average of the replacement fuel cost at Georgia Power's running plants. [P-048] Fixed fuel costs, such as take-or-pay contracts and pipeline fees, stay out of the math. [P-049]
- **Who supports it:** None of the four parties whose testimony we archived asks to keep it unchanged. {analysis: Georgia Power (P-055, P-058), PIA Staff (P-073, P-084), GIPL (P-095, P-096) and GAM (P-111) each propose or accept changes} GAM said that if the PSC finds a change is needed, either of Georgia Power's options would "adequately address" the concerns. [P-111]
- **Who benefits:** More real-time money stays on the base side, where Georgia Power counted $264 million in 2025 toward fixed costs. [P-052] GAM says real-time revenues "place downward price pressure on non-RTP rates." [P-111]
- **Who pays:** Customers on standard rates keep paying the fixed fuel costs left out of the credit. [P-063] PIA Staff estimates that leaving out take-or-pay costs under-counted the fuel credit by $70 million, $74 million and $76 million in 2023, 2024 and 2025. [P-078]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether the real-time money on the base side makes up for the fuel costs left out. {editorial} Georgia Power says it does. [P-124] GIPL says that money pays for more efficient power plants and is not extra savings for other customers. [P-098]

### Option B: Georgia Power's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 1, which takes both the cost and the energy of take-or-pay contracts out of the fuel credit math. [P-058, P-128] New pipeline fees caused by very large new customers would be recovered from them, using each group's share of winter peak growth to split the fees. [P-055, P-056] Other left-out costs stay out. [P-131]
- **Who supports it:** Georgia Power and GIPL back Option 1. [P-128] GIPL backs the new pipeline idea only with changes. [P-101] GAM says either of Georgia Power's options would work. [P-111]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit than today. [P-058, P-115] Georgia Power says Option 1 keeps real-time pricing "a marginal cost-based rate." [P-129] It says its pipeline plan protects other customers even if planned large projects fall through. [P-127]
- **Who pays:** Less real-time money goes toward base costs, which GAM says puts "upward pressure on base rates." [P-115] Very large new customers' payments would cover the new pipeline fees tied to their growth. [P-055]
- **What's uncertain:** How much Option 1 would add, because Georgia Power's estimates for both options are blacked out in the public copy. [P-138, P-139, P-140] GIPL says Georgia Power has not let other parties review the cost model behind its winter peak split. [P-103]

### Option C: PIA Staff's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 2, which counts both the cost and the energy of take-or-pay contracts. [P-073] Shares all pipeline and gas storage fees, old and new, among all customers by monthly energy use. [P-157] If the PSC prefers a peak-based split, Staff would instead move all pipeline fees from the fuel charge to base rates. [P-086] Counts other fuel-related costs too, such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead. [P-087, P-088] Staff makes no recommendation on three costs it calls policy-related, such as gas hedging gains and losses. [P-089] Adds monthly reports starting no later than Jan 1, 2027. [P-068]
- **Who supports it:** PIA Staff. [P-073] No other party's testimony we read backs Option 2. {analysis: Georgia Power and GIPL back Option 1 (P-128); GAM accepts either option (P-111)}
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit than today. [P-079, P-080] PIA Staff estimates Option 2 alone would add about $126 million to the credit in 2026 and about $399 million in 2031. {computed: Table 6 "Delta" column (P-079): $125,870,149 for 2026 and $399,271,411 for 2031, rounded to the nearest million} PIA Staff said that if its plan had applied in 2023–2025, the added money would have wiped out a $322 million shortfall in the fuel account. [P-066] It said today's fuel rate would then be lower by about $1.35 a month for a home using 1,000 kWh. [P-066, P-067]
- **Who pays:** Less real-time money goes toward base costs, which GAM says puts "upward pressure on base rates." [P-115] Part of the pipeline and storage fees would be counted against real-time payments, based on energy use. [P-157] PIA Staff estimated Georgia Power's return on equity would have been an average of 0.49 percent lower in 2023–2025, still within the range the PSC allows. [P-091] Return on equity is a measure of the company's profit. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **What's uncertain:** Georgia Power calls Staff's three guidelines "new policy recommendations." [P-120, P-118] PIA Staff says its data were incomplete, so its estimates may be too low. [P-078] Many of the figures behind Staff's tables are blacked out in the public copy. [P-079] Staff's Oct 7 corrections changed figures in several of its tables. [P-039]

### Option D: GIPL's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 1 for take-or-pay contracts. [P-095, P-128] New pipeline fees caused by very large new customers would be recovered from them, split the way transmission costs are split instead of by winter peak. [P-096, P-104] The credit would also include a direct share of pipeline fees, plus coal-related costs and corporate overhead. [P-096]
- **Who supports it:** GIPL, with Park Avenue Baptist Church, Temple Sinai and Unitarian Universalist Church of Savannah. [P-093]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit than today. [P-105] GIPL says this "will assist in reducing FCR rates for FCR customers." [P-105]
- **Who pays:** GIPL says the change "may increase base rates for those same customers, depending on how other cost allocation questions are handled." [P-105] Real-time customers' prices would not change. [P-105]
- **What's uncertain:** GIPL's pipeline advice assumes Georgia Power's capacity prices don't already include pipeline costs, and GIPL offers two other choices if they do. [P-096] Georgia Power's rebuttal says GIPL's witness switched to an energy-based split at a hearing. ### 1. Leave take-or-pay contracts out entirely (Option 1)

Documented · Side: favors Option 1 · Claim type: mostly values (how a real-time rate should work) · Evidence: Supported, with limits (no party disputes that it raises the fuel credit; the amount is blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Georgia Power and GIPL say real-time customers should carry only costs they can change by using more or less power, so the contracts should be left out of the math entirely. [P-130, P-100] This raises the fuel credit, but by how much is not public. [P-128, P-139]
- **Who says it:** Georgia Power witnesses Ross Beppler, Lee Evans and Brandon Looney said in rebuttal testimony on Oct 27, 2026, that "Option 1 preserves the integrity of RTP as a marginal cost-based rate." [P-117, P-129] GIPL witness John D. Wilson said Option 1 "resolves this problem with the current RTP fuel credit calculation." [P-092, P-100, P-128]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power's direct testimony said removing the contracts' energy from the math "will increase the RTP fuel credit rate." [P-058] Georgia Power said GAM and PIA Staff agree that Option 1 raises the credit. [P-128] GIPL said that while the contracts' output stays below standard-rate customers' use, Option 1 gives those customers both the contracts' costs and their benefits. [P-100, P-128]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said Option 1 "omits non-dispatchable costs from the calculation when the energy from these non-dispatchable resources serve all customers." [P-074]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power's own direct testimony said Option 1 "is not based on all Georgia Power generating units running, as originally approved." [P-058, P-128] PIA Staff said that if Georgia Power wanted a true marginal cost, it should have used the "associated interchange energy rate" instead. [P-075]
- **What's missing:** The public copies we found have no dollar figure for what Option 1 would add. [P-138, P-139]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether keeping real-time pricing tied to marginal cost matters more than counting every cost that serves all customers; this is mainly a question of values. {editorial}

### 2. Count the costs of take-or-pay contracts too (Option 2)

Documented · Side: favors Option 2 · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Supported, with limits (PIA Staff's estimate from Georgia Power data, which Staff calls incomplete; Georgia Power's own estimates are blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says take-or-pay contracts serve all customers, so their costs should count when the fuel credit is figured. [P-080] Staff estimates that leaving the costs out under-counted the credit by $220 million in 2023–2025, an estimate based on data Staff calls incomplete. [P-078]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn, in testimony filed Sep 23, 2026. [P-060, P-160, P-161, P-059] They said: "These costs are incurred to serve all customers at all times and should be included when the Company calculates the amount of RTP revenues credited to fuel revenues." [P-080]
- **What supports it:** In 2025, about 9.8 million MWh of take-or-pay energy was counted in the calculation while the related $486 million in costs was not, PIA Staff said. [P-076] That $486 million is the contracts' total cost for the year, not the share real-time customers would carry. [P-077] Georgia Power's own direct testimony said Option 2 would "result in a larger allocation of RTP revenues to the fuel clause." [P-058, P-128]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said Option 2 "would be a departure from the marginal fuel cost-based approach approved by the Commission and in place since 2010." [P-129] GIPL said leaving the contracts out is "appropriate, as incremental RTP load should be assigned the cost of resources that may be dispatched in response to price-sensitive demand." [P-100]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said real-time customers cannot avoid take-or-pay costs by cutting their use. [P-130] It said the contracts' fixed prices lower average fuel costs for customers on standard rates when those prices are below marginal fuel cost. [P-130]
- **What's missing:** Georgia Power's own estimates for Option 1 and Option 2 are blacked out in the public copy. [P-138, P-139, P-140] So the public copies we found have no dollar figure for Option 1 to compare with Staff's Option 2 figures. {analysis: P-139 is fully blacked out; Staff's figures (P-078, P-079) cover Option 2 only}
- **What's uncertain:** How big the gap really is, since Staff said its results "may actually under-estimate the total impact." [P-078]

### 3. Tie new pipeline fees to peak demand (Georgia Power's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors Georgia Power's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (Georgia Power, GAM and PIA Staff call the fees fixed but split them differently; the cost model behind Georgia Power's split is not public) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Georgia Power says pipeline fees are fixed years ahead and driven by peak demand, so new fees caused by very large customers should be split by winter peak growth, not by energy use. [P-122, P-126, P-055, P-056] PIA Staff and GIPL disagree with this way of splitting the fees. [P-085, P-101]
- **Who says it:** Georgia Power's witnesses said energy use "is not an appropriate allocation factor for FT because FT is fixed, driven by peak demand." [P-126] GAM witness Jonathan Ly said no part of real-time revenue should be credited against these fees "because FT costs are fully fixed costs that affect GPC’s average cost of service." [P-108, P-110]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power said its split is fixed in advance, so very large customers cover the new fees even if their projects don't materialize. [P-127] GAM said the plan "would ensure that incremental FT costs attributable to new very large load customers are fully recovered from these customers." [P-113]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said: "FT is necessary for all hours across each month in order to reliably serve customers." [P-085] GIPL said it does not agree "that it is reasonable to excuse incremental RTP load from an explicit allocation of FT cost." [P-101]
- **What challenges it:** GIPL said Georgia Power has not let other parties review the large-load cost model behind the split, "not even on a trade secret basis." [P-103] PIA Staff said the plan leaves out existing pipeline fees and "adds undue complexity." [P-082]
- **What's missing:** The dollar amounts in Georgia Power's forecast of new pipeline fees are blacked out in the public copy. [P-146]
- **What's uncertain:** How much new pipeline cost very large customers will cause, since those amounts are not public. {analysis: P-146}

### 4. Share all pipeline and storage fees by energy use (PIA Staff's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors PIA Staff's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Mixed (Staff and Georgia Power disagree on whether the company could collect some costs twice; how to split fixed costs is partly a values question) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says pipeline and storage fees are needed in every hour, so all of them should be shared by monthly energy use, real-time customers included. [P-085, P-157] Staff and Georgia Power disagree on whether the company could otherwise collect some of these costs twice. [P-083, P-123]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff said its approach is "consistent with how FT costs have been recovered from non-RTP customers over the past 15 years." [P-084]
- **What supports it:** PIA Staff said real-time customers use power "in both high and low load hours." [P-081] GIPL said splitting by energy or by peak demand gives different results for different customer groups, and it called for a direct pipeline share in the credit. [P-102]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said it would only collect twice "if it was charging customers for FT costs in both fuel and base rates," and that pipeline fees are not in base rates. [P-123] GAM said no specific part of real-time revenue should be credited against pipeline fees. [P-110]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said Staff's own fuel-case testimony five months earlier used a peak-based method. [P-125] PIA Staff said it used that method "simply for illustration purposes." [P-145]
- **What's missing:** PIA Staff's yearly estimates for its pipeline plan are blacked out in the public copy. [P-147]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether real-time money on the base side already helps pay these fees, as Georgia Power says. [P-124] GIPL called that "questionable." [P-148]

### 5. Other fuel costs left out of the credit fall on other customers (PIA Staff and GIPL)

Documented · Side: favors counting more costs (PIA Staff and GIPL) · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Supported that the costs are left out (the parties agree); not mainly a factual question whether they should count <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff and GIPL say fuel costs such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead are left out of the credit, which shifts them to customers on standard rates. [P-063, P-099] Georgia Power agrees these costs are not in the credit but says they should stay out. [P-049, P-131]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff said leaving out these costs "results in non-RTP customers absorbing all these costs." [P-063] Staff based its view on three guidelines, including that fuel costs should be recovered from all customers based on energy use. [P-062] GIPL's witness said the credit is "an underestimate of the fuel costs that should be allocated to the incremental load priced at RTP." [P-099]
- **What supports it:** PIA Staff said costs like rail car leases depend on how much fuel, or what kind, the company buys. [P-149] GIPL said coal-related costs rise and fall with lasting changes in demand. [P-156]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said the PSC "should decline to adopt PIA Staff’s recommendations presented in Table 1." [P-131] It said Staff's guidelines are "new policy recommendations" that "are not based on or consistent with cost-allocation best practices." [P-120] It said "the RTP rate is by design a marginal cost-based rate that is not intended to recover all embedded costs." [P-121]
- **What challenges it:** The two supporters split on some items: GIPL would leave gas resale gains and losses out, while PIA Staff would count them. [P-106, P-087] PIA Staff itself said it does not necessarily expect every left-out cost to be included. [P-064]
- **What's missing:** In PIA Staff's public table of the costs left out of the credit, most single items are blacked out. [P-150]
- **What's uncertain:** Which costs should count as fuel costs at all; this is partly a question of how to define them. {editorial}

### 6. A bigger fuel credit means less help with base costs (GAM)

Documented · Side: accepts either of Georgia Power's options (GAM) · Claim type: factual and predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits (the PSC's order and three parties' testimony say base rates may be affected; the size is unknown and GAM's figures are blacked out) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** GAM says real-time revenue already lowers other customers' base costs, so a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates." [P-111, P-115] How much base rates might change would be settled in the next base rate case. [P-016]
- **Who says it:** GAM witness Jonathan Ly, for the Georgia Association of Manufacturers, whose members use large amounts of power. [P-108, P-109] Georgia Power said real-time revenue above added costs puts "downward pressure on rates for all customers." [P-045]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power's direct testimony said changing the method would change base rate revenue. [P-047] The PSC's order sends any matching base-side change to the next base rate case. [P-016] GIPL agrees the shift "may increase base rates for those same customers, depending on how other cost allocation questions are handled." [P-105] A cost study GAM cited shows homes pay a bigger share of base revenues, 58.07%, than of fuel revenues, 55.36%. [P-116] Georgia Power estimates $100 million more in base rates would raise a typical home's bill a bit more than $100 million less in fuel charges would lower it. {computed: about $1.65 versus $1.48 a month per $100 million (P-142, P-143)}
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said any base-rate impact "would be a natural consequence of revising the methodology to accurately match costs and cost recovery in the same mechanism." [P-090] GIPL said the base-side money "does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098]
- **What challenges it:** PIA Staff found Georgia Power could have absorbed the larger fuel credit in 2023–2025 while staying within the profit range the PSC allows. [P-090, P-091] So base rates would not necessarily rise. {analysis: follows from P-090 and P-091}
- **What's missing:** GAM's figures for how much real-time revenue lowered base costs in 2025 are blacked out in the public version. [P-112, P-114]
- **What's uncertain:** How base rates will change, which the next base rate case decides. [P-016] Georgia Power says base rates are frozen through 2028. [P-054]

### Questions to weigh

- Should big customers on hourly prices help pay fuel costs that don't change with how much power they use? {editorial}
- How should the PSC weigh a lower fuel charge now against possibly higher base rates later? {editorial}
- How should the cost of new gas pipeline space be shared between very large new customers and everyone else? {editorial}
