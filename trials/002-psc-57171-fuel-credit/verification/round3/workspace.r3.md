## Layer 2: The decision

**What's being decided:** Georgia Power splits money from real-time customers between its fuel account and its base account. [P-061] The part counted toward fuel, called the fuel credit, lowers what other customers pay for fuel. [P-144] The PSC is reviewing how that split is figured. [P-016]

**What would change:**

1. **Take-or-pay contracts**, power Georgia Power must pay for whenever it is produced: today the credit counts their energy but not their cost. [P-050, P-049, P-072, P-076] Option 1 (backed by Georgia Power and GIPL) would remove both, and Option 2 (PIA Staff) would count both; either one raises the credit. [P-058, P-128, P-129, P-115]
2. **Pipeline fees**, fixed payments to reserve space on gas pipelines: Georgia Power, PIA Staff and GIPL each filed a different way to count them. [P-051, P-081, P-055, P-157, P-104] GAM says no specific part of real-time money should count against them. [P-110]
3. **Other fuel costs**, such as coal rail car leases and corporate overhead: PIA Staff and GIPL would count them, and Georgia Power would leave them out. [P-065, P-096, P-131]
4. **Reports:** PIA Staff recommends monthly reports on the credit, starting no later than Jan 1, 2027; Georgia Power says they aren't needed and some aren't feasible in that time. [P-068, P-132]

### How real-time pricing works

- Business customers with a demand of at least 250 kilowatts can choose real-time pricing (RTP). [P-044, P-042] About 2,300 use the day-ahead version (RTP-DA), and about 170 use the hour-ahead version (RTP-HA), which requires at least 5 megawatts. [P-044, P-164]
- They pay standard rates for a set baseline amount of power and an hourly price for power above it. [P-043] The hourly price reflects what Georgia Power expects its marginal cost to be. [P-042] Marginal cost is the cost of making one more unit of power. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- Georgia Power says these customers take on price risk in exchange for possible savings. [P-042]
- Georgia Power expects most new business growth to use real-time pricing, PIA Staff said. [P-070] GIPL says the method under review has been applied to data centers. [P-097]
- PIA Staff says new large-load customers on one rate schedule (tariff) have no baseline, pay real-time rates and pay a monthly access charge to cover added fixed costs. [P-071] GAM says new customers of 100 megawatts and up may choose real-time pricing for all or part of their power, and pay access charges that reflect Georgia Power's added costs. [P-168] The two descriptions may cover different groups. {analysis: P-071 describes customers on one tariff; P-168 describes customers of 100 MW and up}
- GIPL said real-time customers' prices would not change under its plan, only how the money is counted. [P-105] Georgia Power said the same about its 2010 change to the method. [P-046] Real-time customers pay standard rates on their baseline power. [P-043] So a later base-rate change would reach that part of their bills. {analysis: follows from P-043}

### Words used on this page

- **Fuel charge:** the part of a standard-rate bill that pays for fuel; its formal name is fuel cost recovery (FCR). [P-021, P-061]
- **Base rates:** the rest of the bill, which pays for power plants, power lines, other costs besides fuel, and the company's profit. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Base rate case:** the PSC's full review of base rates. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Take-or-pay contracts:** contracts, such as Georgia Power's solar, wind and biomass deals, under which it must pay for power whenever it is produced. [P-050] The filings also call them non-dispatchable PPAs, or power purchase agreements. [P-050, P-119]
- **Pipeline fees:** fixed monthly payments to reserve space on gas pipelines for Georgia Power's power plants. [P-049, P-051] The filings call them firm transportation (FT). [P-096]
- **Peak demand and energy use:** peak demand is the most power used at one time, such as on a cold winter morning; energy use is the total power used over a month. {analysis: plain-language definitions, not from the cited sources}
- **Winter CP:** a split based on each group's use at the hour of the winter peak; CP stands for coincident peak. {analysis: plain-language gloss; P-056 uses "1-CP (coincident peak)" and "winter CP"}
- **Average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost:** the rate the credit uses today. [P-048] It is the cost to replace the fuel burned, averaged over all of Georgia Power's units running that hour. {analysis: plain-language gloss of P-048}
- **System lambda:** the cost of the last unit of power the system makes in an hour. [P-052] It is part of the real-time price, and Georgia Power says it is above the average running cost in nearly all hours. [P-052]
- **Capital substitution:** Georgia Power's name for part of the gap between lambda and the money recorded to fuel. [P-052] It goes to base rates, and Georgia Power says it helps pay fixed costs such as plants and pipeline fees. [P-052]
- **Large-load customers:** the term Georgia Power and PIA Staff use; the passages we read give it no size threshold. {editorial: process note; P-051, P-055 and P-071 use the term without one} GAM's "very large load" means new customers of 100 megawatts and up. [P-168]
- **Large-load cost model:** Georgia Power's model of the costs of serving large-load customers. {analysis: plain-language gloss; P-056 names the model}
- **Under-collected fuel balance:** fuel costs already spent but not yet collected through the fuel charge. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **Incremental load:** the power billed at real-time prices. [P-043]
- **GPC:** Georgia Power Company. [P-109]
- **Embedded costs:** the full average cost of running the system, as opposed to marginal cost. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- **kWh and MWh:** a kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the unit on a power bill, and a megawatt-hour (MWh) is 1,000 of them. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}

### Fuel side versus base side

- Under the PSC's order, any change is credited to fuel. [P-016] The matching base-side change is recorded as "a regulatory asset or liability" and dealt with in the next base rate case. [P-016] The order does not say which way base rates would move. {analysis: P-016 provides for either an asset or a liability}
- Georgia Power says the benefits of adding large customers let it freeze base rates through 2028. [P-054] It says their added revenue will put downward pressure of at least $102 a year on a typical home's bill in 2029–2031. [P-054] This is Georgia Power's projection, and no other party's estimate of it is public. {analysis: no passage gives another estimate} It covers all revenue from large-load customers, not only the real-time money at issue here. {analysis: P-054 describes "the incremental revenue from large load customers"}
- If fuel charges had to collect $100 million less, a typical home's bill would drop by about $1.48 a month, Georgia Power estimates. - GAM says a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates." ## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

Below are today's method and the three plans in the testimony we could read. {editorial: process note; Resource Supply Management's testimony is a scanned image that could not be copied into passages} The PSC can also mix parts of them. {analysis: the order says the PSC "may make modifications if appropriate" (P-016)} The plans use letters; Option 1 and Option 2 are only the two take-or-pay choices. {editorial} Only Plan C's take-or-pay part has public estimates of what it would add to the credit. [P-078, P-079] Georgia Power's estimates for Option 1, used in Plans B and D, are blacked out. [P-139] A hidden figure is not zero. {editorial}

### Plan A: Keep today's method

- **What it does:** Counts real-time money toward fuel at the "average hourly marginal replacement fuel cost" of Georgia Power's running plants, plus its share of "pool sales or purchases." [P-048] It counts take-or-pay contracts' energy but not their cost, and leaves pipeline fees out. [P-049, P-058, P-076]
- **Where it stands:** Georgia Power, PIA Staff and GIPL propose changes. [P-055, P-058, P-073, P-095] GAM proposes none: it argues against a bigger credit, and accepts either take-or-pay option only if the PSC finds a change is needed. [P-111, P-115] Georgia Power says real-time customers already pay "their fair share of system costs." [P-121]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates, through lower base costs, Georgia Power and GAM say. [P-124, P-111] They say real-time money not counted toward fuel helps pay fixed costs on the base side. [P-124, P-111] Georgia Power estimates that one part of that money, which it calls capital substitution, came to $264 million in 2025. [P-052]
- **Who pays:** PIA Staff and GIPL say customers on the fuel charge carry the fixed fuel costs left out of the credit; GIPL says this "shifts costs to FCR customers." [P-063, P-099] PIA Staff estimates that Option 2 would have added $70 million, $74 million and $76 million to the credit in 2023, 2024 and 2025. [P-078] Staff says the data Georgia Power provided were incomplete, so these figures may be too low. [P-078] They are measured against Option 2; Georgia Power's estimates for Option 1 are blacked out. [P-078, P-139]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether the real-time money on the base side makes up for the fuel costs left out. {editorial} Georgia Power says it helps pay fixed costs such as pipeline fees, and GAM says it can. [P-124, P-110] GIPL agrees with the idea, but says it "neglects two critical factors." [P-099] It says fuel and base costs are shared among customer groups differently, and fuel balances carry forward. [P-099]

### Plan B: Georgia Power's plan

- **What it does:** Uses Option 1, removing both the cost and the energy of take-or-pay contracts. [P-058, P-128] New pipeline fees tied to large-load customers would be recovered from them, based on their share of growth in winter peak demand. [P-055, P-056] Existing pipeline fees and the other costs now left out would stay out of the credit. [P-065, P-131]
- **Where it stands:** Georgia Power proposes it and prefers Option 1. [P-119, P-128] GIPL also backs Option 1 and taking new large-load pipeline fees out of the fuel charge. [P-128, P-096] But it rejects the winter-peak split and would count coal and overhead costs. [P-101, P-096] PIA Staff opposes Option 1 and the exclusion of existing pipeline fees, though it also counts new pipeline fees in the credit. [P-074, P-082, P-065] GAM says that if a change is needed, either take-or-pay option would "adequately address" the concerns. [P-111]
- **Who benefits:** Customers on standard rates get a bigger fuel credit, from Option 1 and from new pipeline fees counted against large-load customers' payments. [P-058, P-055] Georgia Power says its pipeline split protects other customers even if planned large projects fall through. [P-127]
- **Who pays:** Less real-time money goes toward base costs, so base rates could rise later. [P-115, P-105] The parties differ on whether and how much, as Layer 2 explains. [P-115, P-105, P-090] More of large-load customers' payments would be counted toward the new pipeline fees tied to their growth. [P-055, P-127]
- **What's uncertain:** How much Option 1 would add: Georgia Power's estimates for both options are blacked out in the public copy. [P-138, P-139, P-140] The dollar amounts in its forecast of new pipeline fees are blacked out too. [P-146] Georgia Power says its split is consistent with its large-load cost model. [P-056] GIPL says that claim "is not supported by any available evidence in this or any other related proceeding." [P-103] It says intervenors have not been allowed to review the model. [P-103]

### 1. Remove take-or-pay contracts' cost and energy from the credit (Option 1)

Documented · Side: favors Option 1 · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Main claim: Mixed (Georgia Power and GIPL back Option 1, each for its own reason; PIA Staff says the contracts' energy serves all customers). Size: no public estimate; Georgia Power's estimates are blacked out, though the parties agree Option 1 raises the credit <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Georgia Power and GIPL say both the contracts' cost and their energy should come out, which raises the credit; PIA Staff disagrees. [P-058, P-128, P-074]
- **Who says it:** Georgia Power witnesses Ross Beppler, Lee Evans and Brandon Looney filed rebuttal testimony on Oct 27, 2026. [P-117] They said "Option 1 preserves the integrity of RTP as a marginal cost-based rate." [P-129] They said real-time customers cannot avoid take-or-pay costs by changing their use. [P-130] GIPL witness John D. Wilson filed direct testimony on Sep 23, 2026. [P-092, P-152] Wilson said real-time load should carry the costs of plants that are run up or down as demand responds to prices: it "should be assigned the cost of resources that may be dispatched in response to price-sensitive demand." [P-100]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power said removing the contracts' energy "will increase the RTP fuel credit rate," and that PIA Staff and GAM agree Option 1 raises the credit. [P-058, P-128] It said the contracts and their costs "are not driven by large load growth." [P-050] It said "RTP load pays the marginal fuel cost and does not directly benefit from the fixed price feature of the non-dispatchable PPA." [P-130] It said the PSC approved in 2010 recording real-time fuel revenue at "average marginal replacement fuel costs (rather than the embedded FCR rate)." [P-046] GIPL said that while the contracts' output stays below standard-rate customers' use, Option 1 gives those customers both the contracts' costs and their benefits. [P-100, P-128]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said Option 1 "omits non-dispatchable costs from the calculation when the energy from these non-dispatchable resources serve all customers." [P-074]
- **What challenges it:** PIA Staff said an accurate marginal-cost method would use the "associated interchange energy rate," another way to measure marginal cost. [P-075] Georgia Power's direct testimony said Option 1 "is not based on all Georgia Power generating units running, as originally approved." [P-058] In the same passage, Georgia Power said this choice "is consistent with an approach that the RTP fuel credit should be derived from marginal replacement fuel costs." [P-058]
- **What's missing:** The public copies we found have no dollar figure for what Option 1 would add. [P-138, P-139]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether keeping real-time pricing tied to marginal cost matters more than counting every cost that serves all customers; this is mainly a question of values. {editorial}

### 2. Count the costs of take-or-pay contracts too (Option 2)

Documented · Side: favors Option 2 · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Main claim: Mixed (PIA Staff says the contracts' energy serves all customers; Georgia Power and GIPL say their costs and benefits go to customers on standard rates). Size: PIA Staff's estimate is the only public one; Georgia Power's is blacked out <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says the contracts serve all customers, so their costs should count in the credit; Georgia Power and GIPL disagree. [P-080, P-129, P-100]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn filed testimony on Sep 23, 2026. [P-059, P-060, P-160, P-161] They said: "These costs are incurred to serve all customers at all times and should be included when the Company calculates the amount of RTP revenues credited to fuel revenues." [P-080]
- **What supports it:** In 2025, about 9.8 million MWh of take-or-pay energy was counted in the calculation while the related $486 million in costs was not, PIA Staff said. [P-076] Staff said this "unreasonably suppressed" the credit. [P-076] That $486 million is the contracts' total cost for the year, not the share real-time customers would carry. [P-077] Staff said the excluded contracts may top 17 million MWh by 2027, "showing that the impacts of non-dispatchable PPAs are growing." [P-077] PIA Staff estimates that Option 2 would have added $220 million to the credit in 2023–2025. [P-078] Staff says the data Georgia Power provided were incomplete, so the figure may be too low, and it projects the difference would keep growing through 2031. [P-078, P-079] Georgia Power's direct testimony said Option 2 would "result in a larger allocation of RTP revenues to the fuel clause." [P-058]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said real-time customers cannot avoid take-or-pay costs by cutting their use, and that the contracts' fixed-price benefits go to customers on standard rates. [P-130] GIPL said leaving the contracts out is "appropriate, as incremental RTP load should be assigned the cost of resources that may be dispatched in response to price-sensitive demand." [P-100]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said Option 2 "would be a departure from the marginal fuel cost-based approach approved by the Commission and in place since 2010." [P-129] PIA Staff said Option 2 "is consistent with the 2010 rate case proposal, which assumed that all Georgia power unit and purchase costs would be included in the calculation." [P-073]
- **What's missing:** Georgia Power's estimates for both options are blacked out in the public copy. [P-138, P-139, P-140]
- **What's uncertain:** How the two options compare in size, since only Option 2 has a public estimate. {analysis: P-078 and P-079 cover Option 2; P-139 is blacked out} Whether counting every cost that serves all customers matters more than keeping real-time pricing tied to marginal cost; this is mainly a question of values. {editorial}

### 3. Recover new pipeline fees from large-load customers, split by winter peak (Georgia Power's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors Georgia Power's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Main claim: Mixed (no party disputes that large-load growth drives new fees; GIPL also ties pipeline space to peak demand but rejects a single winter peak; PIA Staff says the fees are needed in every hour). Size: no public estimate; Georgia Power's new-fee forecast is blacked out <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Georgia Power says large-load growth is a primary driver of new pipeline fees, so those customers should pay their share, set by winter peak demand; PIA Staff and GIPL disagree. [P-051, P-056, P-085, P-101]
- **Who says it:** Georgia Power witnesses Ross Beppler, Lee Evans and Brandon Looney filed direct testimony on Aug 14, 2026. [P-041] They proposed that new pipeline fees tied to large-load customers "be fully recovered from those customers and not borne by non-large load customers in the FCR clause." [P-055] In rebuttal testimony filed Oct 27, 2026, they said pipeline fees are fixed and driven by peak demand, like power plants and power lines, so energy use "is not an appropriate allocation factor for FT." [P-117, P-126]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power said "the growth of large load customers is a primary driver of new FT costs." [P-051] It said its split builds on PIA Staff's peak-based showing in the fuel case: "The Company agrees with this approach but proposes using a winter CP." [P-056] PIA Staff says it used that showing "simply for illustration purposes." [P-145] Georgia Power said its split is fixed in advance, so it covers the new fees tied to large-load customers even if their projects don't materialize. [P-127] GIPL's filed testimony also said pipeline space "is required to meet peak demand conditions whenever and for whatever reason they may occur." [P-104] GAM, describing the plan, said it "would ensure that incremental FT costs attributable to new very large load customers are fully recovered from these customers." [P-113]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said: "FT is necessary for all hours across each month in order to reliably serve customers." [P-085] GIPL agreed that taking new large-customer fees out of the fuel charge "addresses one problem," but it rejected the winter-peak split and said real-time load should not be excused from "an explicit allocation of FT cost." [P-101] GAM said no specific portion of real-time revenue should be credited against pipeline fees "because FT costs are fully fixed costs that affect GPC’s average cost of service." [P-110] It said, "In contrast, RTP is a marginal cost-based rate." [P-110] It said the pipeline debate "appears to conflate RTP" with pricing for new very large customers, who "will pay access charges that reflect GPC’s incremental costs." [P-168]
- **What challenges it:** PIA Staff said the plan leaves out existing pipeline fees and "adds undue complexity." [P-082] Georgia Power said it treats existing fees separately because they "are not driven by large load growth." [P-051] Georgia Power said its split is "consistent with the capacity need that drove the incremental generation resources and the allocation in the large load cost model." [P-056] GIPL said that claim "is not supported by any available evidence in this or any other related proceeding." [P-103] It said intervenors have not been allowed to review the model, "not even on a trade secret basis." [P-103]
- **What's missing:** The dollar amounts in Georgia Power's forecast of new pipeline fees are blacked out in the public copy. [P-146]
- **What's uncertain:** How much new pipeline cost large-load customers will cause, since those amounts are not public. {analysis: P-146} How to split fixed costs between customer groups is partly a values question. {editorial}

### 4. Share all pipeline and storage fees by energy use (PIA Staff's pipeline plan)

Documented · Side: favors PIA Staff's pipeline plan · Claim type: factual and values · Evidence: Main claim: Mixed (PIA Staff says the fees serve every hour and its split avoids collecting them twice; Georgia Power agrees the pipelines serve all periods but says peak demand drives the cost, and denies double collection). Size: no public estimate; PIA Staff's yearly estimates are blacked out <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** PIA Staff says all pipeline and storage fees should be shared by energy use, since the fees serve every hour and it sees a risk of double recovery; Georgia Power and GAM disagree. [P-157, P-085, P-083, P-126, P-110]
- **Who says it:** PIA Staff witnesses Tom Newsome, Philip Hayet and Leah Wellborn filed testimony on Sep 23, 2026. [P-059, P-060, P-160, P-161] They said an energy split "is consistent with the manner in which fuel costs are recovered" and with "how FT costs have been recovered from non-RTP customers over the past 15 years." [P-084]
- **What supports it:** Staff's reason is the risk of double recovery. [P-083] It said Georgia Power has said some base-side real-time money "is intended to cover some FT costs," while "all FT costs are indisputably charged to and recovered from fuel revenues." [P-083] Staff said its plan is meant "to eliminate this risk." [P-084] PIA Staff said real-time customers use power "in both high and low load hours." [P-081] Georgia Power's direct testimony describes the fees as ensuring pipeline availability "during all periods." [P-051]
- **Who disagrees:** Georgia Power said Staff's energy split "does not provide this level of customer protection" if large projects fall through. [P-127] It said pipeline fees are "fixed, driven by peak demand," like other large infrastructure costs. [P-126] It said it would only collect twice "if it was charging customers for FT costs in both fuel and base rates," and that pipeline fees are not in base rates. [P-123] GAM said no specific portion of real-time revenue should be credited against these fees. [P-110] It said real-time sales "can contribute to the recovery of these costs" when system lambda "exceeds GPC’s average fuel costs." [P-110]
- **What challenges it:** Georgia Power said Staff's energy split "cannot be reconciled" with Staff's fuel-case testimony five months earlier, which used a peak-based method. [P-125] PIA Staff had said it used that method "simply for illustration purposes." [P-145]
- **What's missing:** PIA Staff's yearly estimates for its pipeline plan are blacked out in the public copy. [P-147]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether real-time money on the base side already helps pay these fees is disputed: Georgia Power says it does, GAM says it can, and GIPL says lambda does not include pipeline costs. [P-124, P-110, P-148] How to split fixed costs between customer groups is partly a values question. {editorial}

### 5. Split new pipeline fees the way power-line costs are split (GIPL's pipeline plan)

### 8. Limit the shift of real-time money to fuel (GAM)

Documented · Side: favors limiting the shift to fuel (GAM; accepts either take-or-pay option if a change is needed) · Claim type: factual, predictive and values · Evidence: Main claim: Mixed (GAM and Georgia Power say base-side real-time money lowers other customers' costs; GIPL says part of it is a needed cost, not a surplus). Size: no public estimate; a future base rate case would set any change <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** GAM says real-time money already lowers other customers' base costs, so a bigger fuel credit "would put upward pressure on base rates"; PIA Staff and GIPL disagree. [P-111, P-115, P-090, P-098]
- **Who says it:** GAM witness Jonathan Ly filed testimony on Sep 23, 2026. [P-153] Ly said "any increase in the amount of RTP revenues credited to GPC’s fuel account will necessarily reduce the RTP revenues currently used to offset GPC’s base revenue requirements." [P-111]
- **What supports it:** Georgia Power's direct testimony said real-time revenue above added costs puts "downward pressure on rates for all customers." [P-045] Its rebuttal said real-time money on the base side is "effectively lowering the retail cost of service for customers in a base rate case." [P-124] Georgia Power also agreed that changing the method would change base rate revenue. [P-047] GAM said real-time revenue cut other customers' base revenues by $214 million in Georgia Power's 2022 rate case. [P-114] Citing Georgia Power, GAM said system lambda contributed $264 million in 2025, and that the 2025 total is "a significant increase." [P-114] GAM said other parts of the real-time price also help pay fixed operating costs. [P-110] GAM said a bigger fuel credit would help medium and large businesses more than homes, citing Georgia Power's cost study. [P-116]
- **Who disagrees:** PIA Staff said "since the fuel costs are completely the responsibility of ratepayers and the Company has no financial exposure, it is critical to get the RTP fuel credit methodology correct." [P-090] It said any base-rate impact "would be a natural consequence of revising the methodology to accurately match costs and cost recovery in the same mechanism." [P-090] GIPL, answering the same point from Georgia Power, said it agrees "with this concept," but that it "neglects two critical factors." [P-099] GIPL said fuel and base costs are shared among customer groups differently, and fuel over- or under-collections carry forward as a balance. [P-099]
- **What challenges it:** GIPL said capital substitution is "a necessary cost of service, and from an economics perspective does not provide further surplus to represent downward pressure on rates." [P-098]
- **What's missing:** GAM's 2025 total for real-time money on the base side is blacked out in the public version. [P-112, P-114]
- **What's uncertain:** How much base rates would move, and which way: the PSC's order leaves that to Georgia Power's next base rate case. [P-016] Whether a lower fuel charge now is worth a possible base-rate change later is partly a values question. {editorial}

### Questions to weigh

- Some fixed fuel costs, such as take-or-pay contracts and pipeline fees, don't change with hour-to-hour use. [P-049, P-081] Should part of real-time customers' payments be matched against them in the fuel account, or keep counting toward base costs? {editorial}
- How should the PSC weigh a lower fuel charge now against possible base-rate effects later, which its order leaves to the next base rate case? {editorial}
- Should any real-time revenue be counted toward pipeline and gas storage fees? {editorial} If so, should old and new fees be treated alike, split by peak demand or by energy use, or moved to base rates? {editorial}
- Which matters more: keeping the real-time rate tied to marginal cost, or matching fuel-related costs with fuel revenues? {editorial}
- Customer groups pay different shares of fuel costs and base costs. [P-116, P-099] How would each plan affect homes compared with businesses? {editorial}
