# HB 1009 (2026): Phones in Georgia high schools

Status: Law (Act 395) · Signed May 5, 2026 · Takes effect Dec 31, 2026 · Trial 001, revision 4, see verification.md <!-- nocheck -->
How this page was made: an AI agent drafted it from archived sources; every sentence is cited or labeled. <!-- nocheck -->

## 30-second version

- **What it does:** Public high school students won't be allowed to use phones or similar devices during the school day, even between classes. [P-007, P-003, P-002] Schools must start no later than July 1, 2027. [P-007] Students may use a device as their health or disability plan explicitly requires. [P-013]
- **Who it affects:** Public high school students, their families, and the school staff who carry out the rules. [P-007, P-011, P-014]
- **What's next:** The law takes effect Dec 31, 2026. [P-019] Each district sets details, like where phones are kept. [P-008, P-009] You can share your views with your school district or your state lawmakers, who can keep or change the law. {editorial: suggested actions; districts set policies each year under P-008, and the General Assembly can amend laws in later sessions}

## Layer 2: The decision

**What was decided:** Whether to extend Georgia's school-day device ban for public schools from kindergarten through 8th grade to high school. [P-028, P-006]

**What the law says the ban is for:** It defines a "distraction-free education" as one where student devices are banned to reduce distractions, improve school results, and increase meaningful interactions and focused learning. [P-057]

**Before this law:** A 2025 law already bars devices during the school day for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade. [P-006, P-028] That rule had to start no later than July 1, 2026. [P-006]

**What changes for high schools:** The rules that already cover younger students in public schools will apply through 12th grade. [P-007, P-008]

1. Starting no later than July 1, 2027, students won't be allowed to use devices during the school day. [P-007] That means from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-002]
2. Each district chooses where phones go, such as lockers, locked pouches or a set place in the classroom. [P-009]
3. Parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school. [P-011] Emergency messages go through the school's own systems. [P-011]
4. Students whose IEP, Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires a device may use it as the plan requires. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
5. Breaking the rules leads to escalating consequences, such as warnings, taking the device, calling parents and other actions. [P-012]

### Also new in this law

- District policies must be reviewed every year by January 1, instead of by a one-time deadline of January 1, 2026. [P-008, P-020]
- The law now names dual enrollment, work-based learning and apprenticeships as examples of off-campus activities that district rules must cover. [P-010, P-065] Dual enrollment means taking college classes while in high school. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- E-readers were dropped from the law's list of example devices, but that list is not complete. [P-004, P-021]

### Other rules in the law

- Districts must set rules for off-campus activities like field trips, school transportation and sports. [P-010]
- Students learning online away from campus are not covered. [P-005]
- Schools can still give students laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- Schools are encouraged to hold family workshops, town halls and screen-free challenges. [P-060]
- Charter schools and other schools that can usually skip some state rules can't skip this one. [P-018]

## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

### Option A: Keep the old law (no state rule for high schools)

- **What it does:** Sets a device ban for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade only. [P-006, P-066]
- **What happened:** HB 1009, signed May 5, 2026, expands that law to high schools; the change takes effect Dec 31, 2026. [P-024, P-028, P-019]
- **Who benefits:** High school students and families keep whatever phone access their district allows. {analysis: the state rule covered only K-8 (P-006), so grades 9-12 were left to districts} Teacher Darryl Adams said high school students, who are older and often more independent, should be allowed to keep access to their phones. [P-048] Schools avoid new monitoring and enforcement duties and keep local control. {analysis: the duties come from P-014, and P-018 bars opting out} If Florida's results apply here, students would also avoid the short-term rise in suspensions seen there, especially among Black students. {analysis: based on P-039}
- **Who pays:** In districts without limits, phones stay in class, the kind of distraction the new law aims to reduce. {analysis: the K-8-only rule (P-006) left grades 9-12 to districts; the law defines distraction-free education as banning access "to minimize distractions" (P-057)} State School Superintendent Richard Woods called for extending the ban to end the harm he said constant phone access causes. [P-036] If Florida's results apply here, students would miss the test-score and attendance gains found there. {analysis: based on P-040} High school rules could differ from one district to the next. {analysis: follows from leaving grades 9-12 to each district}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether districts would have limited phones on their own. {editorial} Sen. Sally Harrell said, "It has been done in high schools already, it’s been very successful and we look forward to rolling that out statewide." ### Option B: HB 1009 as passed (now law)

- **What it does:** Bans device access for public high school students during the school day, from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-007, P-002]
- **What happened:** It passed the House 145 to 20 and the Senate 52 to 0, and was signed on May 5, 2026. [P-025, P-024]
- **Who benefits:** In Florida, phone bans led to better test scores in the second year, and the study's findings suggest they cut unexcused absences. [P-034]
- **Who pays:** Families won't be able to reach students directly during the school day and will have to contact the school instead. [P-007, P-011] Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8% said there would be no way to reach their child in an emergency. [P-035] Students who use phones or smartwatches for health apps need a formal plan that explicitly requires the device, unless another law requires access. [P-013, P-007, P-004] Schools must set up phone storage methods, monitor and enforce the rules, review policies every year and keep families informed. [P-009, P-014, P-008, P-015] The law gives schools state guidance but sets aside no money; any state funding would come through the budget, which we didn't review. {analysis: no funding provision in the full enacted text, P-001 to P-019 and P-056 to P-061; P-016 provides guidance} In Florida, enforcing phone bans led to more suspensions in the short term, especially among Black students; the rise began to fade after the first year. [P-039]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether, and how much, focus, learning and well-being will change in Georgia high schools, and whether suspensions will rise or emergency contact will suffer, since the evidence so far is limited (see Layer 4). {editorial} How much the new duties will cost, since we found no official cost estimate (fiscal note) and costs depend on each district's choices, such as lockers or locked pouches. {analysis: searched the 2025-2026 fiscal-note list, which has a 2026 section (P-062), for 1009; storage options are listed in P-009}

### Option C: The Senate amendment that failed

- **What it does:** This proposed change, called an amendment, would have let districts allow high school students to use phones in the brief time between classes. [P-027]
- **What happened:** Sen. Kemp of the 38th District offered it, and it failed, 13 to 38. [P-027, P-026] This senator is not Gov. Brian Kemp. {analysis: P-027 identifies "Senator Kemp of the 38th"}
- **Who benefits:** In districts that chose to allow it, students and families could reach each other between classes. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027} Each district would decide for itself. {analysis: under P-027, access "may be permitted in accordance with policies" adopted by local school systems}
- **Who pays:** In those districts, part of the school day would no longer be phone-free. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether phones between classes would change focus or learning compared with a full-day ban, since no source we reviewed measured this. {editorial}

### 1. The ban will help students learn

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits (one Florida working paper designed to estimate cause and effect; gains in year two; no Georgia results yet) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters say fewer distractions mean more learning; a Florida study found phone bans led to better test scores in the second year. [P-029, P-036, P-040]
- **Who says it:** Gov. Brian Kemp said the bills he signed would "reduce distractions in the classroom." [P-029] State School Superintendent Richard Woods said learning without distractions is "both an academic issue and a mental health issue." [P-036] Among parents who support a ban, 87.7% expected better focus and learning. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** In Florida, phone bans led to better test scores in the second year, and the study's findings suggest they cut unexcused absences. [P-040, P-038] Fewer unexcused absences may explain a large share of the test-score gains. [P-040]
- **Who disagrees:** No one in the sources we reviewed disputed this directly. {analysis: none of the archived statements argues that bans hurt learning; 19.8% of opposing parents said phones are needed for schoolwork (P-035), a related concern covered in card 6}
- **What challenges it:** In Florida, the test-score gains came in the second year, after a first year in which suspensions rose. [P-039, P-040] Neither study summary we read reports that bans hurt learning. {analysis: no negative learning result in P-038 to P-043, P-064, P-067 or P-068}
- **What's missing:** The study used data from Florida only.
