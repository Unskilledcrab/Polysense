# HB 1009 (2026): Phones in Georgia high schools

Status: Law (Act 395) · Signed May 5, 2026 · Takes effect Dec 31, 2026 · Trial 001, revision 3, see verification.md <!-- nocheck -->
How this page was made: an AI agent drafted it from archived sources; every sentence is cited or labeled. <!-- nocheck -->

## 30-second version

- **What it does:** Starting no later than July 1, 2027, public high school students won't be allowed to use, hold or wear phones or similar devices during the school day, including time between classes. [P-007, P-003, P-002] Some students with health or disability plans that require a device are exempt. [P-013]
- **Who it affects:** Public high school students, their families, and the school staff who carry out the rules. [P-007, P-011, P-014]
- **What's next:** The law takes effect Dec 31, 2026, and each district sets details like where phones are kept. [P-019, P-008, P-009] You can share your views with your school district, or with your state lawmakers, who can keep or change the law. {editorial: suggested actions; districts set policies each year under P-008, and the General Assembly can amend laws in later sessions}

## Layer 2: The decision

**What was decided:** Whether to extend Georgia's school-day device ban for public schools from kindergarten through 8th grade to high school. [P-028, P-006]

**The law's stated goal:** Fewer distractions, better school results, and more meaningful interactions and focused learning. [P-057]

**Before this law:** A 2025 law already bars devices during the school day for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade. [P-006, P-028] That rule starts no later than July 1, 2026. [P-006]

**What changes for high schools:** The rules that already cover younger students in public schools will apply through 12th grade. [P-007, P-008]

1. Starting no later than July 1, 2027, students won't be allowed to use devices during the school day. [P-007] That means from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-002]
2. Each district chooses where phones go, such as lockers, locked pouches or a set place in the classroom. [P-009]
3. Parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school. [P-011] Emergency messages go through the school's own systems. [P-011]
4. Students whose IEP, Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires a device may use it as the plan requires. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
5. Breaking the rules leads to escalating consequences, such as warnings, taking the device, calling parents and other actions. [P-012]

### Also new in this law

- District policies must be reviewed every year by January 1, instead of by a one-time deadline of January 1, 2026. [P-008, P-020]
- Districts must set rules for off-campus activities like field trips, school transportation and sports. [P-010] Those rules must now also cover dual enrollment, work-based learning and apprenticeships. [P-010, P-065]
- E-readers were dropped from the law's list of example devices, but that list is not complete. [P-004, P-021]

### Other rules in the law

- Students learning online away from campus are not covered. [P-005]
- Schools can still give students laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- Schools are encouraged to hold family workshops, town halls and screen-free challenges. [P-060]
- Charter schools and other schools that can usually skip some state rules can't skip this one. [P-018]

## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

### Option A: Keep the old law (no state rule for high schools)

- **What it does:** Sets a device ban for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade only. [P-006, P-066]
- **What happened:** HB 1009, signed May 5, 2026, replaces it when the new law takes effect on Dec 31, 2026. [P-024, P-028, P-019]
- **Who benefits:** High school students and families keep whatever phone access their district allows. {analysis: the state rule covered only K-8 (P-006), so grades 9-12 were left to districts} Teacher Darryl Adams said high school students, who are older and often more independent, should be allowed to keep access to their phones. [P-048] Schools avoid new monitoring and enforcement duties and keep local control. {analysis: the duties come from P-014, and P-018 bars opting out}
- **Who pays:** Students in districts without limits keep their phones in class. {analysis: follows from the K-8-only rule, P-006} Woods called for extending the ban to end the harm he said constant phone access causes. [P-036] If Florida's results apply here, students would miss the test-score and attendance gains a ban might bring. {analysis: based on P-040} High school rules could differ from one district to the next. {analysis: follows from leaving grades 9-12 to each district}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether districts would have limited phones on their own. {editorial} Sen. Sally Harrell said such rules had "been done in high schools already." [P-046] We found no count of districts that already had them. {analysis: none in the sources reviewed}

### Option B: HB 1009 as passed (now law)

- **What it does:** Bans device access for public high school students during the school day, from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-007, P-002]
- **What happened:** It passed the House 145 to 20 and the Senate 52 to 0, and was signed on May 5, 2026. [P-025, P-024]
- **Who benefits:** In a Florida study, test scores rose in the second year of school phone bans, unexcused absences fell, and effects were larger in middle and high schools. [P-040] Among parents who back a ban, 87.7% expected better focus and learning, and 60.3% better physical health and safety. [P-034] Sen. Sally Harrell said legislators want to hear teachers say, "I can just teach again." [P-045] Woods told lawmakers he had heard nothing but support from teachers across Georgia. [P-053]
- **Who pays:** Families can't reach students directly during the school day and must contact the school instead. [P-007, P-011] Students who use phones or smartwatches for health apps need a formal plan that explicitly requires the device, unless another law requires access. [P-013, P-007, P-004] Schools must set up phone storage methods, monitor and enforce the rules, review policies every year and keep families informed. [P-009, P-014, P-008, P-015] The law gives schools state guidance but sets aside no money, and we found no official cost estimate (fiscal note) for it. {analysis: no funding provision in the full enacted text, P-001 to P-019 and P-056 to P-061; P-016 provides guidance; searched the 2025-2026 fiscal-note list, which has a 2026 section (P-062), for 1009} In Florida, enforcing phone bans led to a significant short-term rise in suspensions, especially among Black students, which began to fade after the first year. [P-039]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether, and how much, focus, learning and well-being will change in Georgia high schools, since the evidence so far is limited (see Layer 4). {editorial}

### Option C: The Senate amendment that failed

- **What it does:** Would have let districts allow high school students to use phones during short breaks between classes. [P-027]
- **What happened:** Sen. Kemp of the 38th District offered it, and it failed, 13 to 38. [P-027, P-026] He is a state senator, not Gov. Brian Kemp. {analysis: P-027 identifies "Senator Kemp of the 38th"}
- **Who benefits:** In districts that chose to allow it, students and families could reach each other between classes. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **Who pays:** In those districts, part of the school day would no longer be phone-free. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether phones between classes would change focus or learning compared with a full-day ban, since no source we reviewed measured this. {editorial}

### 1. Phone-free school days help students learn

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits (one Florida working paper; gains in year two, possibly through better attendance) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters say fewer distractions mean more learning; a Florida study found test scores rose in the second year of phone bans. [P-029, P-036, P-040]
- **Who says it:** Gov. Brian Kemp said the bills he signed would "reduce distractions in the classroom." [P-029] State School Superintendent Richard Woods said learning without distractions is "both an academic issue and a mental health issue." [P-036] Among parents who support a ban, 87.7% expected better focus and learning. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** In Florida, test scores rose in the second year of school phone bans, unexcused absences fell, and the effects were larger in middle and high schools. [P-040] Fewer unexcused absences may explain a large share of the test-score gains. [P-040]
- **Who disagrees:** No one in the sources we reviewed disputed this directly. {analysis: none of the archived statements argues that bans hurt learning}
- **What challenges it:** We found no evidence against this claim, and the English study didn't measure test scores. {analysis: neither archived study reports a negative learning effect; the English study's outcomes are listed in P-064}
- **What's missing:** The gains came only in the second year, after a first year with more suspensions. [P-039, P-040] The study used data from Florida only. [P-038]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether Georgia high schools will see results like Florida's, since we found no Georgia high school results yet. {analysis: none in the sources reviewed; Georgia's rule isn't required until July 1, 2027 (P-007)}

### 2. Less phone time helps teens' mental health and friendships

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Uncertain (one comparison of 12- to 15-year-olds in England found no well-being difference; it couldn't test cause and effect) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters expect better mental health and friendships; the one study we found, of younger students in England, found no evidence of a well-being difference. [P-034, P-042]
- **Who says it:** Woods called for extending the ban "to end the harm caused by constant technology access and interruption." [P-036] The bill's sponsor, Rep. Scott Hilton, said, "The technology meant to bring people together actually makes us feel further apart." [P-051] Students testified in support at the hearing; one told lawmakers that phones have become addictive for teens. [P-052] Among parents who support a ban, 71.2% expected better in-person social interactions and 69.1% better mental health. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** Hilton said districts had reported less isolation among younger students under all-day phone limits. [P-051] In the English study, strict rules went with about 40 minutes less phone time and about 32 minutes less social media time during school. {computed: 0.67 hours × 60 ≈ 40 minutes; 0.54 hours × 60 ≈ 32 minutes (P-042)}
- **Who disagrees:** The English study's authors said there is "no evidence that restrictive school policies are associated with overall phone and social media use or better mental wellbeing in adolescents." [P-043]
- **What challenges it:** That study found no evidence of better well-being at schools with strict rules, or of less phone use overall. [P-042, P-043]
- **What's missing:** The district reports Hilton described were about younger students. [P-051] The article gives no measurements behind them. {analysis: P-051 reports the claim without data} The English study can show links, not cause and effect, and it didn't measure friendships. {analysis: cross-sectional design (P-041); its outcomes are listed in P-064}
- **What's uncertain:** How Georgia teens' well-being and friendships will change, because no study we found tested a rule like Georgia's on high schoolers. {editorial: reason drawn from P-041, P-042 and P-064}

### 4. Families lose direct contact with students during the school day

Documented · Side: concerned about the law · Claim type: factual, with a values question · Evidence: Supported on the facts (the law's text); uncertain on emergency safety (no data on school alerts) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Students can't use phones during the school day, so families must reach them through the school. [P-007, P-011] How well school alerts work in a crisis is unknown. {analysis: no data on alert performance in the sources reviewed}
- **Who says it:** Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8% said there would be no way to reach their child in an emergency. [P-035] Teacher and parent Darryl Adams said phones can be critical for families seeking information during emergencies. [P-049]
- **What supports it:** Under the law, parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school directly. [P-011] The law has no emergency exception for students' own devices. {analysis: the full enacted text (P-001 to P-019, P-056 to P-061) allows access only under a plan that requires a device (P-013) or where access is "required by law" (P-007); neither is an emergency exception} Adams said that when two of his daughters were in a lockdown, he could not get updates for hours. [P-049]
- **Who disagrees:** Sen. Sally Harrell said, "We’ve gotten very dependent on being able to reach our kids whenever we want to, but that education time needs to be protected." [P-044] Among parents who support a ban, 60.3% expected better physical health and safety. [P-034]
- **What challenges it:** An Emory public health professor, Julie Gazmararian, said, "Parents’ concerns about emergency access are valid and understandable. - **What's missing:** The law doesn't require the rapid notification or guaranteed phone access Gazmararian described, though districts can add them because the law sets minimum rules. {analysis: no such requirement in the full enacted text; P-008 says policies must "at a minimum" include its list} Adams's lockdown account is one family's experience; the source doesn't say when it happened or what phone rules applied. {analysis: based on P-049}
- **What's uncertain:** How well school alert systems will reach families in a real crisis, since our sources have no data on how they perform. {editorial}
