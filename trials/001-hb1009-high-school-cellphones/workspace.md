# HB 1009 (2026): Phones in Georgia high schools

Status: Law (Act 395) · Signed May 5, 2026 · Trial 001, revision 2, see verification.md <!-- nocheck -->
How this page was made: an AI agent drafted it from archived sources; every sentence is cited or labeled. <!-- nocheck -->

## 30-second version

- **What it does:** Public high school students can't use, hold or wear phones or similar devices during the school day, from the first bell to the last, including time between classes. [P-007, P-003, P-002] There are exceptions for some students with health or disability plans that require a device. [P-013]
- **Who it affects:** Students in public high schools, their families, and the school staff who carry out the rules. [P-007, P-011, P-014]
- **What's next:** Schools must start by July 1, 2027, at the latest. [P-007] Each district decides details like where phones are kept. [P-008, P-009] You can share your views with your school district, or with your state lawmakers, who can change laws in future sessions. {editorial: suggested actions; districts set policies each year under P-008, and the General Assembly can amend laws in later sessions}

## Layer 2: The decision

**What was decided:** Whether to extend Georgia's school-day device ban for public schools from kindergarten through 8th grade to high school. [P-028, P-006]

**Before this law:** A 2025 law already barred public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade from using devices during the school day, starting no later than July 1, 2026. [P-006, P-028]

**What changes for high schools:** The rules that already covered younger students in public schools now apply through 12th grade. [P-007, P-008]

1. Students can't use devices from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes, starting no later than July 1, 2027. [P-007, P-002]
2. Each district chooses where phones go, such as lockers, locked pouches or a set place in the classroom. [P-009]
3. Parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school, and emergency messages go through the school's own systems. [P-011]
4. Students whose IEP, Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires a device may use it as the plan requires. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
5. Breaking the rules leads to escalating consequences, such as warnings, taking the device, calling parents and other actions. [P-012]

### Also new in this law

- District policies must be reviewed every year by January 1, instead of by a one-time deadline of January 1, 2026. [P-008, P-020]
- Districts must set procedures for off-campus programs, now including dual enrollment, work-based learning and apprenticeships. [P-010]
- E-readers were dropped from the law's list of example devices, but that list is not complete. [P-004, P-021]

### Still true

- Students learning online away from campus are not covered. [P-005]
- Schools can still give students laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- Charter schools and other schools that can usually skip some state rules can't skip this one. [P-018]

## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

### Option A: Keep the law as it was (no state rule for high schools)

- **What it meant:** State law set a device ban for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade only. [P-006, P-020]
- **What happened:** It was replaced when HB 1009 was signed on May 5, 2026. [P-024, P-028]
- **Who benefits:** High school students and families keep whatever phone access their district allows, including direct contact during the day. {analysis: the state rule covered only K-8 (P-006), so grades 9-12 were left to districts} Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8% named emergency contact as a reason. [P-035] Schools avoid new monitoring and enforcement duties. {analysis: those duties come from P-014 under HB 1009}
- **Who pays:** State School Superintendent Richard Woods said extending the ban would "end the harm caused by constant technology access and interruption." [P-036] High school rules could differ from one district to the next. {analysis: follows from leaving grades 9-12 to each district}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether districts would have limited phones on their own, since we found no count of districts that already did. {editorial}

### Option B: HB 1009 as passed (now law)

- **What it does:** Bans device access for public high school students during the school day, from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-007, P-002]
- **What happened:** It passed the House 145 to 20 and the Senate 52 to 0, and was signed on May 5, 2026. [P-025, P-024]
- **Who benefits:** Supporters expect students to focus better; 87.7% of parents who back a ban gave that reason. [P-034] Sen. Sally Harrell said legislators want to hear teachers say, "I can just teach again." [P-045]
- **Who pays:** Families can't reach students directly during the school day and must contact the school instead. [P-007, P-011] Schools must store phones, monitor and enforce the rules, review their policies every year and keep families informed. [P-009, P-014, P-008, P-015] In Florida, suspensions rose in the short term after phone bans, especially among Black students. [P-039] The law itself doesn't set aside money for these duties; any state funding would come through the budget, which we didn't review. {analysis: no funding provision appears anywhere in the enacted text, P-001 to P-019 and P-056 to P-061} We found no official state cost estimate (called a fiscal note) for HB 1009 on the budget office's 2025-2026 list, which has a 2026 section. {analysis: searched the list (S10c) for 1009 and found none; P-062 shows its 2026 section; a missing entry doesn't prove no estimate exists}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether, and how much, focus, learning and well-being will change in Georgia high schools, since the evidence so far is limited (see Layer 4). {editorial}

### Option C: The Senate amendment that failed

- **What it would have done:** Let districts allow high school students to use phones during short breaks between classes. [P-027]
- **What happened:** It failed, 13 to 38. [P-026]
- **Who benefits:** Students and families could reach each other between classes. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **Who pays:** Part of the school day could stop being phone-free, in districts that chose to allow phones between classes. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether phones between classes would change focus or learning compared with a full-day ban, since no source we reviewed measured this. {editorial}

## Layer 4: Arguments you'll hear

In a survey of 1,002 Georgia parents in fall 2025, 70.8% supported a high school ban and 29.2% opposed it. [P-032, P-033] The margin of error is about 4 points either way. {computed: 1.96 × 2.1 standard error (P-033) ≈ 4.1 percentage points}

Two studies appear below: a Florida study released as a 2025 working paper, and an English study of 30 schools. [P-055, P-041] A working paper is an early research report shared before formal publication. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources} We read each study's summary, not the full paper. {editorial: process note}

### 1. Phone-free school days help students learn

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits (one Florida study) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters say fewer distractions mean more learning, and a Florida study found test scores rose in the second year of phone bans. [P-029, P-036, P-040]
- **Who says it:** Gov. Brian Kemp said the bills he signed would "reduce distractions in the classroom." [P-029] State School Superintendent Richard Woods said learning without distractions is "both an academic issue and a mental health issue." [P-036] Among parents who support a ban, 87.7% expected better focus and learning. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** In Florida, test scores rose in the second year of school phone bans, and the effects were larger in middle and high schools. [P-040] The study was designed to estimate cause and effect, not just links. [P-038]
- **Who disagrees:** No one in the sources we reviewed disputed this directly. {analysis: none of the archived statements argues that bans hurt learning}
- **What challenges it:** The English study's summary lists well-being and phone use as its outcomes, not test scores. [P-064] Its authors said their findings "do not provide evidence to support" school-day phone bans in their current form. [P-043]
- **What's missing:** Fewer unexcused absences may explain a large share of Florida's test-score gains. [P-040] The gains came in the second year, after a first year with more suspensions. [P-039, P-040]
- **What's uncertain:** Whether Georgia high schools will see results like Florida's. {editorial} Georgia's rule isn't required until July 1, 2027, though at least one school started early. [P-007, P-054] We found no Georgia high school results yet. {analysis: none in the sources reviewed}

### 2. Less phone time helps teens' mental health and friendships

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Uncertain (no benefit found in the one study reviewed) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters expect better mental health and more in-person connection, but the one study we found saw no well-being difference. [P-034, P-042]
- **Who says it:** The bill's sponsor, Rep. Scott Hilton, said, "The technology meant to bring people together actually makes us feel further apart." [P-051] Among parents who support a ban, 71.2% expected better in-person social interactions and 69.1% better mental health. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** Hilton said districts reported "improved focus and less isolation among younger students" under all-day phone limits. [P-051]
- **Who disagrees:** No one in the sources we reviewed disputed this directly. {analysis: none of the archived statements argues against this claim}
- **What challenges it:** In the English study, students at schools with strict rules used phones about 40 minutes less during school {computed: 0.67 hours (P-042) × 60 ≈ 40 minutes}, but the study found no evidence of better well-being. [P-042] It also found no evidence of less phone use overall. [P-043]
- **What's missing:** The district reports Hilton described were about younger students. [P-051] They are reports, not measured results. {analysis: no data accompanies them in P-051} The English study compared schools at one point in time. [P-041] Studies like this show links, not cause and effect. {analysis: general limit of cross-sectional designs} Its students were ages 12 to 15, in England. [P-042, P-041]
- **What's uncertain:** How Georgia teens' well-being will change, because the only study we found looked at younger students in England. {editorial: reason drawn from P-041 and P-042}

### 3. Phone limits are already working in Georgia

Documented · Side: supports the law · Claim type: factual · Evidence: Uncertain (reports, not measured results) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Supporters point to the 2025 law for younger grades and to schools that already limit phones. [P-028, P-046] None of them cited measured results. {analysis: no data appears in P-028, P-045, P-046, P-051 or P-053}
- **Who says it:** The governor's office said HB 1009 "builds on the success of HB 340," the 2025 law for kindergarten through 8th grade. [P-028] Sen. Sally Harrell said, "It has been done in high schools already, it’s been very successful." [P-046]
- **What supports it:** Harrell said legislators want to hear teachers say, "I can just teach again." [P-045] Woods told lawmakers he had heard nothing but support from teachers across Georgia, according to Atlanta News First. [P-053]
- **Who disagrees:** No named critic disputed that the rules work; teacher Darryl Adams's concerns were about enforcement (see card 5). {analysis: based on P-047 to P-049}
- **What challenges it:** The high school ban was signed on May 5, 2026, before the statewide K-8 deadline of July 1, 2026. [P-024, P-006] So statewide K-8 results weren't available yet. {analysis: follows from the two dates}
- **What's missing:** No source we reviewed measured how the K-8 ban is working. {analysis: none of the archived sources reports K-8 outcome data}
- **What's uncertain:** How well the K-8 rules work statewide, because they only had to start in 2026. {editorial: reason from P-006}

### 4. Families lose direct contact with students during the school day

Documented · Side: concerned about the law · Claim type: factual, with a values question · Evidence: Supported, with limits (the law's text; no data on crisis alerts) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Students can't use phones during the school day, so families must reach them through the school. [P-007, P-011]
- **Who says it:** Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8% said there would be no way to reach their child in an emergency. [P-035] Teacher and parent Darryl Adams said phones can be critical for families seeking information during emergencies. [P-049]
- **What supports it:** Under the law, parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school directly. [P-011] Adams said that when two of his daughters were in a lockdown, he could not get updates for hours. [P-049] An Emory public health professor, Julie Gazmararian, said parents' concerns about emergency access are "valid and understandable." [P-037]
- **Who disagrees:** Sen. Sally Harrell said, "We’ve gotten very dependent on being able to reach our kids whenever we want to, but that education time needs to be protected." [P-044]
- **What challenges it:** The law requires emergency communication to go through school systems run by staff. [P-011] Gazmararian said policies can address the concern with "rapid parent notification systems and guaranteed access to phones when needed." [P-037]
- **What's missing:** The law doesn't require the rapid notification or guaranteed phone access Gazmararian described, and it sets no time limit for reaching parents. {analysis: checked the full enacted text (P-001 to P-019, P-056 to P-061); its communication rules are P-011 and P-059}
- **What's uncertain:** How well school alert systems will reach families in a real crisis, since our sources have no data on how they perform. {editorial}

### 5. Enforcing the ban will burden school staff and cause conflict

Documented · Side: concerned about the law · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits (one Florida study; staff workload not measured) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Critics say enforcement will fall on teachers and administrators and lead to confrontations; in Florida, suspensions rose at first. [P-047, P-039]
- **Who says it:** Teacher Darryl Adams said he worries the bill would put the burden on teachers and administrators to police phones, a task that can invite conflict. [P-047] He said, "We have a lot of older teachers that do not want to have that confrontation." [P-047]
- **What supports it:** Each school system must monitor and enforce the rules, which may include assigning staff and auditing phone storage. [P-014] Consequences can include taking students' devices. [P-012] In Florida, suspensions rose in the short term after phone bans, especially among Black students. [P-039]
- **Who disagrees:** Supporters such as Sen. Sally Harrell say similar rules have worked in other high schools. [P-046]
- **What challenges it:** In Florida, the rise in suspensions began to fade after the first year. [P-039] Districts choose their own storage and enforcement methods. [P-009, P-050]
- **What's missing:** No source we reviewed measured teacher or staff workload. {analysis: none of the archived sources includes workload data}
- **What's uncertain:** How much work each district's approach will create, since district policies were not part of our sources. {editorial}

### 6. High school students are old enough to manage their own phones

Documented · Side: concerned about the law · Claim type: mostly values · Evidence: Not mainly a factual question <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Critics say older students should keep some phone access, as a family choice and a way to learn responsible use. [P-048, P-035]
- **Who says it:** Teacher Darryl Adams said high school students, who are older and often more independent, should be allowed to keep access to their phones. [P-048] A Senate amendment from the senator for the 38th District would have let districts allow phones between classes for grades 9 to 12, and it failed, 13 to 38. [P-027, P-026]
- **What supports it:** Among parents who oppose a ban, 45.5% said it should be the parents' choice and 41.9% said students need to learn responsible use. [P-035] Another 26.6% said phones are needed for health tools or apps, but the law allows a device only when a student's IEP, Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires it. [P-035, P-013]
- **Who disagrees:** A 17-year-old student who testified told lawmakers that phones have become addictive for teens and said, "Social media is the cigarette of our generation." [P-052]
- **What challenges it:** In Florida, the effects of phone bans were larger in middle and high schools. [P-040]
- **What's missing:** Our sources don't measure whether teens learn responsible phone use with or without a ban. {analysis: none of the archived sources addresses this}
- **What's uncertain:** This is mainly a disagreement about who should decide and what school is for. {editorial}
