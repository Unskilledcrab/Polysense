# House Bill 1009 (2026): Phones in Georgia public high schools

## 30-second version

- **What it does:** House Bill (HB) 1009 will bar public high school students from accessing phones, smartwatches, tablets and similar devices during the school day, unless a disability or medical plan explicitly requires it, or another law does. [P-054, P-007, P-003, P-004, P-005, P-002, P-013] Georgia lawmakers and the governor approved the law. [P-056, P-024]
- **Who it affects:** Public high school students, their families and school staff. [P-007, P-011, P-014]
- **What's next:** High schools must start the ban by July 1, 2027. [P-007] Districts set the details. [P-008] Ask your district about its rules, or tell lawmakers whether to keep or change the law. {editorial: suggested actions; districts set policies each year under P-008, and the General Assembly can amend laws in later sessions}

## Layer 2: The decision

**What was decided:** Whether to extend Georgia's school-day device ban for public schools from kindergarten through 8th grade to high school. [P-028, P-006]

**How the law describes distraction-free education:** A learning environment where student access to personal devices is banned "to minimize distractions, improve academic outcomes, and increase meaningful interactions and focused learning experiences." [P-057]

**Before this law:** A 2025 law, House Bill 340, already bars device access during the school day for public school students in kindergarten through 8th grade. [P-006, P-028] That rule had to start no later than July 1, 2026. [P-006]

**What changes for high schools:** The rules that already cover younger students in public schools will apply through 12th grade. [P-007, P-008]

1. Starting no later than July 1, 2027, students won't be allowed to access devices during the school day. [P-007] The ban covers phones, smartwatches, tablets, headphones and similar devices. [P-004] To "access" a device means to view, hold, wear or otherwise use it, for things like messages, going online, games or music. [P-003] The school day runs from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes, a period the law calls "bell-to-bell." [P-005, P-002]
2. Each school district and public school chooses where phones go, such as lockers, locked pouches or a set place in the classroom. [P-008, P-009] The law calls a school district a "local school system." [P-008]
3. Parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school. [P-011] In an emergency, school staff coordinate all messages through the school's emergency systems. [P-011] Because the law says "all," the rule covers messages from the school and between families and students. {analysis: P-011 covers "all emergency communication"}
4. Students whose IEP (Individualized Education Program), Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires a device may access it as the plan requires. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources} Access that another law requires is also allowed. [P-007]
5. Breaking the rules leads to consequences that grow with each violation, such as warnings, taking the device, notifying parents and other actions. [P-012] Each district's or school's code of conduct sets the exact steps. [P-012]

### Also new in this law

- Once the law takes effect on Dec 31, 2026, each school district and public school must adopt its policy, revising it as needed, by January 1 each year. [P-008, P-019] The old law set a one-time deadline of January 1, 2026. [P-020]
- The new law names dual enrollment, work-based learning and apprenticeships as examples of off-campus activities that district rules must cover. [P-010, P-065] Dual enrollment means taking college classes while in high school. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}
- E-readers were dropped from the law's list of example devices, but that list is not complete. [P-004, P-021]

### Other rules in the law

- Districts and schools must set rules for off-campus activities like field trips, school transportation and sports. [P-010]
- Students learning online away from campus are not covered. [P-005]
- Schools can still give students laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- Schools are encouraged to hold family workshops, town halls and screen-free challenges. [P-060]
- Charter schools and other schools that can usually skip some state rules can't skip this one. [P-018]

- **In short:** Teens' mental health and in-person friendships would improve, supporters say. [P-036, P-051, P-071, P-034] A study in England found no evidence of better well-being among 12- to 15-year-olds at schools that barred phone use for fun. [P-041, P-042, P-043] That study couldn't test cause and effect. {analysis: it compared schools at one point in time (P-041)}
- **Who says it:** Woods called for extending the ban "to end the harm caused by constant technology access and interruption." [P-036] The bill's sponsor, state Rep. [P-051] Students testified in support at a legislative hearing; one, a 17-year-old, compared social media to cigarettes. [P-052] Among parents who support a ban, 71.2% cited better in-person social interactions and 69.1% better mental health as reasons. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** Hilton said districts had reported less isolation among younger students under all-day phone limits. - **Who disagrees:** The English study's authors said there is "no evidence that restrictive school policies are associated with overall phone and social media use or better mental wellbeing in adolescents." [P-043]
- **What challenges it:** The English study also found no evidence of less phone or social media use overall at its strict schools. [P-042, P-043]
- **What's missing:** The district reports Hilton described were about younger students. - **What's uncertain:** Whether, and how, the well-being and friendships of Georgia's public high school students will change. {editorial} No study we found measured well-being among American high school students under a full-day ban; the English study covered ages 12 to 15. {analysis: P-041, P-042, P-064; the Florida summary (P-038 to P-040) reports no well-being results}

### 3. Phone bans are already working in Georgia

### 4. Families lose direct contact with students during the school day

Documented · Side: opposes the law · Claim type: factual, with a values question · Evidence: Supported on the facts (the law's text); uncertain on emergency safety (no data on how school alerts perform, or on whether students' own phones make emergencies safer) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** Under the law, families must go through the school to reach students during the day. [P-007, P-011] Opponents say phones can be critical for families in an emergency. [P-049, P-047, P-035, P-071] Our sources have no data on emergency safety. {analysis: no archived source reports how school alerts or students' own phones perform in emergencies}
- **Who says it:** Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8% cited having no way to reach their child in an emergency as a reason. [P-035] Teacher and parent Darryl Adams said phones can be critical for families seeking information during emergencies. [P-049, P-047]
- **What supports it:** Under the law, parents who need to reach their child during the school day must contact the school directly. [P-011] The law has no emergency exception for students' own devices. {analysis: the full enacted text (P-001 to P-019, P-056 to P-061) allows access only under a plan that requires a device (P-013) or where access is "required by law" (P-007); neither is an emergency exception} Adams said that when two of his daughters were in a lockdown, he could not get updates for hours. [P-049, P-047]
- **Who disagrees:** State Sen. Sally Harrell said, "We’ve gotten very dependent on being able to reach our kids whenever we want to, but that education time needs to be protected." [P-044]
- **What challenges it:** Julie Gazmararian, a professor at the Rollins School of Public Health, said, "Parents’ concerns about emergency access are valid and understandable. However, effective cellphone policies can address this by establishing rapid parent notification systems and guaranteed access to phones when needed." [P-037] The law requires each district's and school's policy to set clear protocols that send all emergency communication through school emergency systems, coordinated by school staff. [P-008, P-011]
- **What's missing:** The law doesn't require the "rapid parent notification systems" or the "guaranteed access to phones when needed" that Gazmararian named, and her words don't say whose phones. {analysis: no such requirement in the full enacted text (P-001 to P-019, P-056 to P-061); her words are from P-037} Districts can add faster parent alerts, because the law sets minimum rules, but by our reading they can't let students use their own phones in an emergency unless a student's disability or medical plan, or another law, requires access. {analysis: P-008 says policies must "at a minimum" include its list; P-007 allows access only as the law provides, P-011 sends all emergency communication through school systems, and the only device exception is a plan that requires one (P-013)} The quoted passage doesn't say when Adams's lockdown happened or what phone rules applied. {analysis: based on P-049, with P-047 naming him as a teacher}
- **What's uncertain:** How well school alert systems will reach families in a real crisis, and whether students' own phones make emergencies safer or not, since our sources have no data on either question. {editorial}

### 6. High school students should be allowed to keep phone access

Documented · Side: opposes the law · Claim type: mostly values · Evidence: Not mainly a factual question (who should decide; whether teens learn responsible use with or without a ban is untested) <!-- nocheck -->

- **In short:** High school students should be allowed to keep phone access, opponents say, because teens are older and often more independent and the choice should be parents'. [P-048, P-047, P-035] The disagreement is mainly about values. {editorial}
- **Who says it:** Teacher Darryl Adams said high school students, who are older and often more independent, should be allowed to keep access to their phones. [P-048, P-047] Among parents who oppose a ban, 45.5% cited parental choice and 41.9% cited students' need to learn responsible use as reasons. [P-035]
- **What supports it:** Among parents who oppose a ban, 26.6% cited needing phones for health tools or apps and 19.8% cited needing them for schoolwork. [P-035]
- **Who disagrees:** Students testified in support of the ban, and a 17-year-old said, "Social media is the cigarette of our generation." [P-052] Most parents surveyed, 70.8%, supported a high school ban. [P-033]
- **What challenges it:** The law lets a student access a device that their Individualized Education Program (IEP), Section 504 plan or medical plan explicitly requires, but only "as necessary to fulfill the requirements" of that plan. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources} Schools can also still provide laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- **What's missing:** Our sources don't measure whether teens learn responsible phone use with or without a ban. {analysis: none of the archived sources addresses this}
- **What's uncertain:** The disagreement here is mainly about who should decide and what school is for. {editorial}
