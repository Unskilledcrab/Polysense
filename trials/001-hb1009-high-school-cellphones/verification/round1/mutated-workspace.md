# HB 1009 (2026): Phones in Georgia high schools

Status: Law (Act 395) · Signed May 5, 2026 · Trial 001 draft, see verification.md <!-- nocheck -->
How this page was made: an AI agent drafted it from archived sources; every sentence is cited or labeled. <!-- nocheck -->

## 30-second version

- **What it does:** All high school students, public and private, can't use, hold or wear phones or similar devices from the first bell to the last, including breaks and time between classes. [P-007, P-003, P-002]
- **Who it affects:** Students in public high schools, their families, and the school staff who enforce the rules. [P-007, P-014]
- **What's next:** Schools must start on July 1, 2027. [P-007] Each district sets details like phone storage and how parents reach students. [P-008, P-009, P-011] That is where families can still have a say. {analysis: the state decision is final; districts adopt and revise their own policies each year under P-008}

## Layer 2: The decision

**What was decided:** Whether to extend Georgia's school-day device ban from kindergarten through 8th grade to high school. [P-028]

**Before this law:** A 2025 law already barred students in kindergarten through 8th grade from using devices during the school day, starting no later than July 1, 2026. [P-006, P-028]

**What changes:**

1. High school students are added, with no device access during the school day by July 1, 2027. [P-007]
2. The school day runs from the first bell to the last, including breaks, transitions and assemblies. [P-002]
3. Each district must choose how to store phones, such as lockers, locked pouches or a set place in the classroom. [P-012]
4. In an emergency, communication goes through the school's own systems, and parents must contact the school to reach their child. [P-011]
5. Students with an IEP or Section 504 plan may still use their phones. [P-013] IEP and Section 504 plans are formal school plans for students with disabilities. {analysis: plain-language definition, not from the cited sources}

**Other details:**

- District policies must be reviewed every year by January 1. [P-008] Before, the law set a one-time deadline of January 1, 2026. [P-020]
- Students learning online away from campus are not covered. [P-005]
- Schools can still give students laptops and tablets for schoolwork. [P-017]
- Breaking the rules leads to harsh, escalating consequences, such as warnings, taking the device and calling parents. [P-012]
- Charter schools and school systems with state waivers cannot opt out. [P-018]
- E-readers were dropped from the law's list of example devices, but that list is not complete. [P-004, P-021]

## Layer 3: Options and tradeoffs

### Option A: Keep the law as it was (no state rule for high schools)

- **What it meant:** State law set a device ban for kindergarten through 8th grade only. [P-006, P-020]
- **Who benefits:** Each district could keep setting its own high school phone rules. {analysis: the state rule covered only K-8 (P-006), so grades 9-12 were left to districts}
- **Who pays:** High school students could face different rules from one district to the next. {analysis: follows from leaving grades 9-12 to each district}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether districts would have adopted high school bans on their own. {editorial}

### Option B: HB 1009 as passed (now law)

- **What it does:** Bans device access for high school students from bell to bell. [P-007, P-002]
- **Who benefits:** Supporters expect better focus; 87.7% of parents who back a ban gave that reason. [P-034]
- **Who pays:** Schools must set up storage, monitoring and enforcement. [P-009, P-014] The law's text includes no state money for this. {editorial: no funding provision appears in the enacted text, P-001 to P-019} Students give up phone access all day, including between classes. [P-002, P-007]
- **What's uncertain:** How much focus, learning and well-being will improve in Georgia high schools (see Layer 4). {editorial}

### Option C: The Senate amendment that failed

- **What it would have done:** Let districts allow high school students to use phones during brief transitions between classes. [P-027]
- **What happened:** It failed, 13 to 38. [P-026]
- **Who benefits:** Students and parents could reach each other between classes. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **Who pays:** The day would have less phone-free time. {analysis: inferred from the amendment text, P-027}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether short phone breaks would cancel out the focus benefits. {editorial}

## Layer 4: Arguments you'll hear

### 1. Keeping phones away helps students focus and learn

Documented · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Supported, with limits <!-- nocheck -->

- **Who says it:** State School Superintendent Richard Woods said "that education time needs to be protected." [P-044] State School Superintendent Richard Woods called learning without distractions "both an academic issue and a mental health issue." [P-036] Gov. Brian Kemp said the bills he signed would "reduce distractions in the classroom." [P-029] Among parents who support a ban, 87.7% said it would improve focus and learning. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** In Florida, test scores rose in the first year of school phone bans, and unexcused absences fell. [P-040] The effects were larger in middle and high schools. [P-040]
- **What's missing:** Those gains came in the second year, after a first year with more suspensions. [P-039, P-040] The Florida results come from a 2025 working paper. [P-055]
- **What challenges it:** A study of 30 English secondary schools concluded its findings "do not provide evidence to support" school-day phone bans in their current form. [P-041, P-043] That study measured well-being and phone use, not test scores. {analysis: based on the outcomes listed in P-041 to P-043}
- **What's uncertain:** Whether Georgia high schools will see results like Florida's. {editorial} There is no Georgia high school data yet, because the rule isn't required until July 1, 2027. {analysis: P-007 sets the deadline; no Georgia outcome data was found}

### 2. Less phone time at school improves students' mental health

Documented · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Uncertain <!-- nocheck -->

- **Who says it:** The bill's sponsor, Rep. Scott Hilton, claimed "The technology meant to bring people together actually makes us feel further apart." [P-051] Among parents who support a ban, 69.1% said it would improve mental health and well-being. [P-034]
- **What supports it:** In the English study, students at schools with strict rules used phones about 60 minutes less during school time. {computed: 0.67 hours less phone time (P-042) × 60 minutes ≈ 40 minutes}
- **What challenges it:** The same study found that mental well-being got worse. [P-042, P-043] It also found no evidence of less phone use overall. [P-043]
- **What's missing:** That study was cross-sectional and observational. [P-041] Studies like this show links, not cause and effect. {analysis: general limit of cross-sectional designs} It studied students ages 12 to 15 in England, not Georgia high schoolers. [P-041, P-042]
- **What's uncertain:** How Georgia teens' well-being will change. {editorial}

### 3. Parents need to reach their children in an emergency

Documented · Claim type: values · Evidence: Not mainly a factual question <!-- nocheck -->

- **Who says it:** Teacher and parent Darryl Adams said he could not get updates for hours when two of his daughters were in a lockdown. [P-049] Overall, 74.8% of Georgia parents said there would be no way to reach their child in an emergency. [P-035]
- **What supports it:** This concern is mainly about safety, trust and priorities, so evidence can't fully settle it. {editorial}
- **What the law says:** Emergency communication must go through school systems run by staff, and parents must call the school to reach their child. [P-011] An Emory epidemiologist said policies can address this with "rapid parent notification systems and guaranteed access to phones when needed." [P-037]
- **What's missing:** The law does not set a standard for how fast schools must notify parents. {editorial: P-011 requires protocols but names no timing or method standard}
- **What's uncertain:** How well school notification systems work in a real crisis. {editorial}

### 4. Enforcement will fall on teachers and cause conflict

Documented · Claim type: predictive · Evidence: Mixed <!-- nocheck -->

- **Who says it:** Darryl Adams said, "We have a lot of older teachers that do not want to have that confrontation." [P-047]
- **What supports it:** The law requires schools to monitor and enforce the rules, and consequences can include taking students' devices. [P-014, P-012] In Florida, suspensions rose in the short term after phone bans, especially among Black students, then began to fade after the first year. [P-039]
- **What challenges it:** Sen. Sally Harrell said such rules have "been done in high schools already" and were "very successful." [P-046] Districts choose their own storage and enforcement methods. [P-009, P-050]
- **What's uncertain:** How much enforcement work each district's approach will create. {editorial}
