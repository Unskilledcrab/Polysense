# Content style guide

**Goal:** a reader who gives us 30 seconds leaves knowing what's being decided, who it affects, and what
they can do. Someone who gives us 30 minutes can check every claim.

This guide is **v0.6**: working rules from three trials and usability feedback, not settled policy. Each manual trial and
comprehension round updates it, and changes are recorded at the bottom. After each trial, review the rules
against the [tenets](vision.md#tenets): keep the ones that helped, simplify the ones that didn't, and
retire any that a later trial makes unnecessary.

## 1. Answer first

Every workspace starts with a **30-second version** of three lines:

1. **What it does** (or **What's being decided**): one sentence, with no bill jargon. Name who decides, and say in a
   few words what that body is ("the Public Service Commission, five elected officials who …").
2. **Who it affects:** concrete groups ("people who rent", "K-12 students").
3. **What happens next / what you can do:** a date and an action.

Right after it comes **What it means for you** (section 9): who is affected, in the reader's terms.

People often read only these lines, so each must be right **read on its own**. A sentence that is accurate
in context can mislead here. Give every party's view the same way, or give none and state the facts.

## 2. Length budgets

| Element | Budget |
|---|---|
| 30-second version | ≤ 90 words in all |
| "What it means for you" line | First sentence ≤ 25 words; whole line ≤ 45 words |
| Homepage card | ≤ 25 words + date + one action |
| Layer 2 "What's being decided" | ≤ 80 words + ≤ 5 "What would change" bullets |
| Layer 3 intro | ≤ 60 words |
| Layer 3, per option | ≤ 3 bullets each for *who benefits*, *who pays*, *what's uncertain* |
| Values-guide line ("If you care most about…") | ≤ 35 words |
| Argument card "In short" | ≤ 40 words |
| Layer 4 argument card (collapsed) | ≤ 60 words |
| Sentence | Aim for ≤ 20 words; one idea per sentence |
| Layer 5 source note | One line: "why this source" |

If something doesn't fit, it moves to a deeper layer. It is not crammed in.

## 3. Plain language

- **Reading level:** grade 6–8 for Layers 1–3. A script checks this, and people confirm it in testing.
- **Active voice, present tense, and "you"** where natural ("You can send written testimony").
- **Define jargon in place** the first time it appears, or avoid it. Watch-list:

  substitute, LC number, crossover day, sine die, fiscal note, appropriation, O.C.G.A., first/second
  reader, engrossed, enrolled, local act, ad valorem, millage, homestead exemption, SPLOST.
- **Concrete examples:** "If you rent an apartment, this would…".
- **Name things the way people search for them:** "power bill", not "electric utility rate base".

## 4. Numbers

- Give sources' numbers exactly, and cite them.
- Convert to **human terms** where possible (per household, per student, per month). Mark the conversion
  `{computed}` and show the arithmetic in Layer 5.
- Use ranges when sources give ranges. Never invent precision.
- A blacked-out (redacted) number is unknown, not zero. Say so when only one side's numbers are public.
  Blacked-out words are unknown too: say they are blacked out; don't guess at them or paraphrase around them.
- When money moves between two groups or two parts of a bill, show **both sides** with the same numbers
  (more credit on the fuel charge means less on the base side). Figures for one side alone read as one-sided.
- When something is uncertain, **say why** ("depends on how many districts opt in").

## 5. Neutral wording

- Describe what the bill does before anyone's opinion of it.
- Avoid loaded words. The verification checklist includes a list of them, for example "radical",
  "extreme", "common-sense", "giveaway", "attack on", "slash", "gut", "crack down" and "job-killing".
  Use them only inside a cited verbatim quote.
- Name bills by their number and plain description, not by advocacy nicknames. If a nickname is widely
  used, quote it once in quotation marks.
- Write the same fields for every option and side, and give each its strongest case.

## 6. Sentence accounting (every sentence is one of these)

| Tag | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `[P-012]` | A factual sentence supported by a cited passage | "The bill sets a $500 cap. [P-012]" |
| `{computed: …}` | Arithmetic from cited numbers, with the work shown | "{computed: $12M ÷ 1.6M students ≈ $7.50 per student}" |
| `{analysis}` | Our explanation or synthesis of cited facts | "Together, these changes mainly affect renters." |
| `{editorial}` | Framing that can't be cited, such as "what's missing" or "what's uncertain". The blind source check rates each one valid, questionable or invalid ([verification protocol](../process/verification-protocol.md)) | "The fiscal note does not estimate effects on local budgets." |

- **Absence claims need the whole text.** "The law doesn't say X" or "the study didn't measure X" is
  allowed only when the whole text is archived as passages. Otherwise write "the summary we read reports
  no results on X".

## 7. Quotes and attribution

- Quotes are **verbatim**, with a passage citation. Never paraphrase inside quotation marks.
- American punctuation is allowed: one closing period or comma may sit inside the quotation marks even
  if the speaker's sentence continued. Every word must still match the source.
- Documented arguments name the speaker, their role, the venue and the date.
- When a news report paraphrases someone, say so ("argued", "said he worried"). Don't turn a paraphrase
  into a quote.
- Anticipated arguments use "Some people may argue…" and are never attributed.
- **Never name minors.** Describe them by role.
- **Keep political parties out of it.** Describe people by their role (state senator, superintendent,
  teacher), and arguments by the issue. Say "supporters" and "opponents" of this decision, never "the
  left" or "conservatives". Describe organizations by what they do and where they stand on this
  decision, not by ideology. Mention a party only when a fact can't be understood without it (a
  party-line vote, for example), and then the same way for every side. The aim is to help readers think
  about the issue, not to sort them into teams.
- **Pronouns:** use a pronoun for a person only when a source gives it. Otherwise repeat the name or the
  role ("this senator").
- **Timing words matter.** "No later than July 1" is not "on July 1". Keep the law's own precision.
- **Subgroup statistics:** say who was asked ("Among parents who oppose a ban, 74.8%…"), never let a
  subgroup figure read as everyone.

## 8. Argument cards: one bar for every side

Learned from Trial 001, where the bias panel failed twice: first against supporters, then, after
overcorrecting, against critics.

- **One template, same order:** In short · Who says it · What supports it · Who disagrees · What
  challenges it · What's missing · What's uncertain.
- **In short:** what it would mean for people first, then the claim in its side's own terms, then its
  evidence status, in the same form on every card. Don't end one side's summaries with a rebuttal unless
  every card gets one.
- **Who disagrees:** only a statement that answers *this card's* claim. A general remark doesn't count.
  If no source disputes the claim, write "No one in the sources we reviewed disputed this claim directly".
- **Evidence label:** a vocabulary word, then its basis in parentheses ("Supported, with limits (one
  Florida working paper; gains in year two)"). Labels follow the evidence and may differ between
  sides. Never equalize them.
- **Use a study for everything it found.** If a study supplies one side's cost, also show the benefits
  it found, and the reverse.
- **Same verbs, same hedges.** "Said" for every speaker. If one outlet's report gets "according to",
  every report from that outlet gets it.
- **Say who is quoted.** If the named voices come mostly from one side, say so, and say it reflects whom
  the sources quoted, not how many people hold each view.
- **Base rates first.** Give the overall split before any figure from one group.

## 9. Reader first

Learned from the first usability feedback: a reader new to the subject couldn't tell what the PSC is, whether
a decision raises or lowers their bill, or what "Mixed" means, and found the cards written for insiders.

- **Write for the person reading.** After the 30-second version, a **What it means for you** section gives
  2–4 lines that start "If you …" ("If you pay a Georgia Power bill at home: …"). The first sentence of
  each line must be right read on its own. Use the condition the source gives ("if the PSC adopts …").
- **Lead with the effect on people**, then who argues it. Attribute any effect a party disputes.
- **Money: direction, size, when, then what isn't known.** "Your fuel charge would likely go down; by how
  much isn't public, because …". Never present an example (such as a per-$100 million figure) as one plan's
  effect.
- **Name things.** No bare "them", "both", "neither", "this" or "it" unless the noun is in the same sentence.
- **Define every institution and party** in plain, cited words at first mention on each tab, including who
  they are ("GAM, a group of manufacturers").
- **Titles say who decides, what changes and when.** A title like "real-time payments and fuel costs"
  doesn't tell a reader what is up for debate.
- **"Decide" is the reader's power.** Use the Decide label only when the reader votes directly. Officials who
  decide go under **Who decides, and how to reach them**.
- **What each side says it protects.** Layer 3 opens with a values guide: one line for each side with a
  documented argument, in the same form, "If you care most about <value>: <speaker, role>, who backs
  <option>, said "<verbatim quote>"." When a side's argument is about something the options share (the
  process that produced a proposal, say), name what it defends or opposes instead of one option. Every side
  with a documented argument gets a line; a guide that quotes only one side reads as a recommendation
  (Trial 003, round 4). The value must be faithful to the quote. An option or side with no documented
  argument gets no line, and the page says so. No recommendation words ("fits", "best", "should",
  "choose"). The reader's own values are never asked for or stored.
